# Chapter 1

# Something Has Changed

## Recognizing When an Aging Parent May Need Help

It usually doesn't start with a phone call from a hospital. More often it starts with a feeling you can't quite defend.

Your mother tells you the same story twice in one visit, and the second time she tells it like it's new. Your father, who has paid every bill on the day it arrived for forty years, mentions that the water company sent a late notice. "Their mistake," he says, and maybe it was. The kitchen is a little less clean than it used to be. There's a dent in the car nobody brings up. On the drive home you replay all of it and then talk yourself out of it. Everybody forgets things. You forget things. Maybe you're looking for trouble.

That back-and-forth, between "something's off" and "I'm overreacting," is where a lot of adult children spend weeks or months. It's an uncomfortable place to be, partly because there's no clear signal telling you when to stop wondering and start doing something. This chapter is about getting out of that loop. Not by deciding what's wrong with your parent, which isn't your job and probably isn't possible from where you're standing, but by getting clear on what you've actually seen and what deserves attention first.

## Why small changes are easy to miss

If you're unsure whether anything has really changed, there are good reasons for that.

Change that happens slowly doesn't announce itself. A parent who has been cooking a little less each month for a year doesn't look different from one week to the next. You adjust to the new normal right along with them, and only when something forces a comparison (a holiday, a photo, a sibling who hasn't visited in a while saying "Is Dad okay?") does the gap become visible.

Most of us also see our parents in short, structured windows. A Sunday phone call. Dinner once a month. A long weekend at the holidays. Those are the situations people are best at managing. Your parent knows you're coming, the house gets picked up, and a two-hour visit doesn't test the same things a full ordinary Tuesday does.

Then there's the fact that almost every individual change has a reasonable explanation, and your parent will often supply it before you can ask. The bill was lost in the mail. The car got dinged in a parking lot. They skipped book club because they were tired of the woman who talks too much. Each explanation may be completely true. What matters is whether the incidents are starting to add up to a pattern.

And parents don't always tell us things. Some of that is privacy, some is pride, and some is a real desire not to worry anyone. Falls are a good example: according to the CDC, about a quarter of adults 65 and older fall each year, but fewer than half of them tell their doctor.¹ If they're not telling their doctor, they may not be telling you either.

None of this means you should assume the worst. It means that "I'm not sure" is a reasonable place to be, and that the way out of it is paying closer attention, not arguing with yourself.

## Where it often starts

There's no single doorway into this. Some families get a clear, sudden event. Others notice a slow drift. Here are some of the more common starting points. You may recognize one, several, or none of them exactly.

**A fall.** Sometimes it's dramatic: an ambulance, an emergency room, a broken wrist. Sometimes it's a story told after the fact. "I slipped in the garage last week, but I'm fine." Even a fall without injury is worth paying attention to, because the CDC notes that falling once doubles a person's chances of falling again, and that many people become afraid of falling afterward and cut back on activity in ways that can make things worse.¹

**A hospital stay.** A parent who went into the hospital managing fine on their own may come home weaker, more tired, or temporarily more confused, with new medications and instructions to follow. A hospitalization is often the first time a family realizes how much a parent was quietly handling and how little room there was for a setback. (Hospital discharge gets a full treatment later in this series; for now, it's enough to recognize it as a common starting point.)

**Missed obligations.** Bills that go unpaid or get paid twice. Appointments missed or confused. Prescriptions that don't get refilled. Birthday cards that stop arriving from a parent who never missed one. These are often the changes adult children notice first, because they leave a trail.

**Changes around the house.** Mail stacking up unopened. Expired food in the refrigerator, or very little food at all. Laundry piling up. A yard or a house that's gone from tidy to neglected. Burned pots. Scrapes and dents on the car. The house is often where daily functioning shows up most honestly.

**Pulling back from people and activities.** A parent who stops going to church, stops calling friends, quits the bowling league, or seems less interested in things they used to enjoy. Withdrawal can have many causes, including grief, hearing loss that makes conversation exhausting, mobility problems that make getting out harder, low mood, or simply a change in preference. Either way, write it down.

**A gradual increase in needing help.** Sometimes there's no single change, just more calls. Could you come look at the computer? Could you drive me to the appointment? Could you read this letter from Medicare and tell me what it means? Each request is small. Added together over a year, they may tell you something about how much your parent is now managing alone, and how much they're relying on you without either of you having said so.

Sudden changes and gradual ones also need to be kept apart, because they call for different responses. A change that appears over hours or a day or two, like new confusion, a sudden inability to walk, or a dramatic shift in behavior, is a different situation from something that's been creeping along for months. We'll come back to that distinction later in this chapter because it matters a great deal.

## Noticing is not the same as knowing why

Here's the most useful idea in this chapter: your job right now is to observe, not to explain.

When we see a parent struggling, our minds jump straight to a cause, and often to the scariest one available. Mom repeated herself, so it must be dementia. Dad seems down, so he must be depressed. Those conclusions feel like understanding, but they're guesses, and they can lead you in the wrong direction.

The same visible change can come from a wide range of causes. The National Institute on Aging lists a number of things besides dementia that can cause memory problems, including medication side effects, thyroid, kidney, or liver problems, infections, depression and anxiety, sleep problems, alcohol misuse, and low levels of nutrients such as vitamin B12, and notes that memory problems from causes like these usually go away once the condition is successfully treated.² A parent who seems confused may have a hearing problem, a new prescription, an infection, or a lot of grief. A parent who's falling behind on housework may be in pain, may have trouble seeing, or may simply be worn out.

You don't need to sort through those possibilities. Doctors do that, with tests and an exam and information you don't have. What you can contribute is something doctors often don't have enough of: a clear account of what's actually been happening day to day.

That's also why it pays to be careful with labels, both in your head and out loud. "Mom has dementia" is a diagnosis. "Mom asked me three times on Sunday whether I'd eaten lunch" is an observation. The second one is more useful to a doctor, harder for anyone to argue with, and a lot easier for your mother to hear.

## Write down what you've noticed

Vague impressions are hard to act on. They're hard to explain to a sibling, easy for a parent to wave away, and nearly useless to a doctor with fifteen minutes. Specific observations are different.

Compare these:

| Vague impression | Specific observation |
| --- | --- |
| "Dad's getting forgetful." | "On Oct. 12 and again on Oct. 19, Dad didn't remember we'd talked on the phone the day before." |
| "Mom isn't eating right." | "Three visits in a row, the fridge had mostly condiments and the milk was expired. Mom said she's 'not that hungry lately.'" |
| "The house is a mess." | "Unopened mail on the dining table going back about six weeks, including two envelopes from the electric company." |
| "Dad's unsteady." | "Dad held onto the counter and the back of the couch to cross the living room. He didn't do that at Thanksgiving." |

The specific versions share a few things. They say what happened rather than what it means. They include a date or a timeframe. They compare the present to how things used to be, which is often the most telling part. And they stick to what you saw or heard yourself, or what someone you trust told you directly.

A few practical suggestions for keeping track:

- Write it down soon. Memory blurs fast, especially when you're worried. A note on your phone in the driveway after a visit is better than a reconstruction three weeks later.
- Record what's going well, too. If your mother forgot an appointment but is still managing her medications perfectly, both facts matter. A record that only lists problems gives a distorted picture.
- Note frequency. Once is an incident. Three times in a month is a pattern.
- Ask others what they've seen, without leading them. A sibling, a neighbor, a friend from church. "Have you noticed anything different with Mom lately?" gets a more honest answer than "Don't you think Mom's slipping?"
- Keep it respectful. You're writing this to help your parent, and there's a good chance you'll share it with them or their doctor. Write it the way you'd be comfortable having them read it.

That last point deserves a moment. There's a difference between paying attention and keeping a secret file. If you find yourself checking bank statements without permission or quizzing the neighbors behind your parent's back, step back. The goal is to understand the situation well enough to have a good conversation with your parent, not to build a case against them.

## Bring your parent into it

When you're worried, it's tempting to move straight to action: call the doctor, rearrange the house, take over the bills. The impulse comes from a good place, but your parent is an adult with the right to make their own decisions, including ones you wouldn't make. Unless they've given someone authority to act for them, or there's an emergency, most of what happens next depends on their participation and consent.

There's a practical reason to involve them, too. Your parent knows things you don't. They may already be aware of the change and have a reason for it. They may be more worried than you are and relieved that someone noticed. Or they may see things very differently, which is worth knowing early rather than after you've made plans around them.

You don't need a big sit-down conversation at this stage. Small, honest, low-pressure openings usually work better. Lead with what you noticed and ask for their view, rather than announcing a conclusion:

- "I noticed there were a couple of late notices on the table. Is the bill paying getting to be a hassle?"
- "You mentioned you slipped in the garage. What happened? Has that happened before?"
- "You haven't been to cards in a while. Is that a choice, or has something made it harder to get there?"
- "Is there anything that's gotten harder lately that you'd like a hand with?"

Then listen more than you talk. If they give an explanation, take it seriously, even if you're not fully convinced. If they don't want to discuss it right now, that's information too, and it doesn't have to be the last word. You can come back to it.

Chapter 4 comes back to these conversations, including what to do when you, your parent, and the rest of the family see things differently, and Chapter 6 looks at how to talk about help your parent might or might not want. For now, the main thing is to start from respect: you're asking, not telling.

## How urgent is this?

Most of what this chapter covers is the slow kind of change, the kind you observe over weeks and bring up with your parent and their doctor in due course. But some situations can't wait, and the next chapter lays out a simple red-yellow-green system for sorting them. The short version:

**Call 911 right away** for anything sudden and serious: signs of a possible stroke, trouble breathing, sudden confusion, loss of consciousness, a fall where your parent is badly hurt or hit their head and is acting differently, or any danger to themselves or someone else.³

**Contact their doctor soon** when memory or thinking changes have lasted more than a few weeks (the National Institute on Aging advises talking with a doctor about noticeable memory changes),² after any fall, or when changes seem to line up with a new medication, an illness, or a hospital stay. Report what you've seen, and leave any decision about medications to the prescriber.

**Keep watching and keep talking** when you've noticed one or two things that could have ordinary explanations, nothing is getting rapidly worse, and your parent is safe day to day.

If your parent agrees, they can call the doctor with you on the line or let you join the appointment. If they'd rather you weren't involved, you can still share what you've noticed and encourage them to bring it up. And if you're ever unsure whether something is an emergency, treat it as one.

## Your first job is understanding

Once you start seeing a situation clearly, it can feel like everything needs fixing at once: the bills, the house, the driving, the doctor, the long-term plan, the conversation with your siblings. That's a lot to put on yourself in a single week, and most of it doesn't have to happen yet.

For now, the work is smaller and more manageable. Notice what's happening. Write it down. Talk to your parent. Act fast on anything urgent and get the non-urgent things in front of the right person. You can't make good decisions about what kind of help your parent needs until you understand what's actually going on, and understanding takes a little time.

It's also fine not to have answers yet. "Here's what I've seen, and here's what I don't know" is an honest, useful place to stand. It's a much better starting point than a confident conclusion that turns out to be wrong.

## Before you turn the page

Pick one thing you've been noticing and write it down the way the specific examples in this chapter are written, with a date, what you actually saw, and how it compares to before. If you can, add one more observation this week. That's the start of a clearer picture, and the rest of this book builds on it.
