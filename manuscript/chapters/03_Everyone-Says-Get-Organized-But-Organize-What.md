# Chapter 3

# Everyone Says "Get Organized," But Organize What?

Someone tells you that you need to get organized. A website says to "gather important documents." So you buy a binder, write your father's name on the spine, and sit down with it. Then nothing happens, because you don't actually know what goes in it. Which documents? Where would they even be? Does "important" include the cable bill? The binder sits empty on the counter for three weeks, and now you feel behind on something you can't even describe.

The advice isn't wrong. It just stops before it gets useful. This chapter fills in the rest: seven areas of your parent's life, what's worth knowing in each one, and how to find it out without turning every visit into an interview.

## What you're actually building

Generic advice to "get organized" fails in a few predictable ways. It doesn't say what to collect. It treats every gap as equally urgent. And it assumes you can simply ask your parent for everything, when they may not know where things are, may be embarrassed about it, or may hear "Where's your will?" as "I'm planning your funeral."

You're really building two things. The first is a picture: the basic facts (names, numbers, documents, appointments) plus a realistic sense of how your parent is doing day to day. "Dad's on a lot of pills" isn't much for a doctor to work with. "Dad takes nine medications from two pharmacies, and two of them changed when he left the hospital" is.

The second is a ranking. You won't sort out seven areas of someone's life in a month, and you don't need to. You need to know which few gaps could cause the most trouble soonest.

One thing to keep in mind throughout: this is not a medical chart. You don't need medical vocabulary or an understanding of every diagnosis. Write things down the way you'd explain them to a friend, and copy exact names and numbers straight off the paperwork. If your notes would make sense to a sibling reading them at a hospital desk, they're doing their job.

## The seven areas

The assessment at the end of this chapter has a line for each item below, so you don't need to memorize any of it. What follows explains why each piece matters.

### 1. Health and medical care

This is usually the biggest area, and the one families most often find half-filled when they need it. You're capturing what's going on medically and who's involved, not trying to understand the medicine.

Start with **what your parent is being treated for**, in the words their doctors use. Copy diagnoses from a visit summary or discharge paper rather than relying on memory; "something with his kidneys" is a start, but the exact name is better. Add **the most recent hospital stay** (when, where, and the reason the hospital gave) and **any recent surgery or procedure**.

Then **who's involved**: the primary care doctor and every specialist, with what each one treats and how to reach them. If anything happens on a regular schedule, such as physical therapy, cardiac rehab, dialysis, infusions, wound care, or regular lab work, write down the days, times, and place, and note any **therapist or home health agency** that comes to the house.

If your parent has been in the hospital or had a procedure recently, find the **discharge instructions**. They often say what to watch for, which appointments to make, and who to call. Write down any **restrictions** they mention ("no lifting more than ten pounds," "no driving for two weeks") along with the date each one ends, because restrictions are easy to forget once someone feels better.

Finally, note which **insurance** plans your parent has and where the cards are kept.

### 2. Medications

List every prescription, over-the-counter medicine, vitamin, and supplement, with the dose and how often it's taken, and note which pharmacy fills each one. Some people use more than one pharmacy without the rest of the family knowing.

Pay special attention to **recent changes**: anything new, stopped, or changed in dose in the past few months, especially after a hospital stay. Write down what changed, when, and who changed it. You're not judging whether the change was right. You're making sure everyone is working from the same list.

The simplest way to build it is often to gather every bottle in one place, including the ones in the bathroom drawer and the car, and photograph each label.

### 3. Daily functioning and physical condition

This area is about how your parent is actually managing, which professionals often find more useful than any list of diagnoses.

Start with **their normal baseline**. How does your parent move, think, eat, and spend the day when things are going well? "Walks the dog a mile every morning." "Does the crossword in pen." "Gets a little foggy in the late evening." That's the yardstick you'll measure every future change against.

Then note anything that's shifted: **mobility** (walking more slowly, holding onto furniture, trouble with stairs or getting up from a chair, a new cane), and **pain, fatigue, appetite, or how much they're drinking**. If any of these changes are sudden, Chapter 2 helps you decide how quickly to call someone.

Professionals often think about everyday tasks in two groups. One is basic self-care: eating, bathing, dressing, using the toilet, getting in and out of bed or a chair. The other is the work of running a life: cooking, shopping, housekeeping, managing medications and money, using the phone, and getting around. For each, the question is simply whether your parent does it independently, with some help, or not at all right now.

Last, list any **medical equipment**: walkers, shower chairs, hospital beds, oxygen, blood pressure cuffs, glucose meters. Note what your parent has, what's been ordered but hasn't arrived, what they've been told they need, and who supplies it.

### 4. Home safety

Walk through the house the way a first-time visitor would, ideally at the time of day your parent is most likely to be moving around in dim light. Loose rugs, clutter on the floor, stairs without sturdy railings, a dark path to the bathroom, a tub without grab bars, untested smoke detectors, a phone that can't be reached from the floor. You're not redesigning anything. You're writing down what you see. Chapter 6 looks at daily life at home in more detail.

### 5. Transportation

Is your parent still driving, and how is it going? New dents, tickets, or getting lost on familiar routes are worth noting. Just as important: how do they get to medical appointments? If there's a regular treatment or therapy schedule, someone has to get them there. Note who does it now, who would cover if that person couldn't, and whether a doctor's driving restriction after surgery has left a gap.

### 6. Finances

You're looking for the outline only: where income comes from, which bills are regular, where statements go, and what's on autopay. Watch for unopened mail, past-due notices, or unusual withdrawals. Money is a sensitive subject, and this book doesn't go into managing it. Chapter 7 covers keeping track of where financial records are.

### 7. Legal documents

Find out whether your parent has a health care power of attorney (sometimes called a health care proxy), a financial power of attorney, a living will, and a will, and where the signed originals are kept.

This area deserves attention even when everything else looks calm, because timing can matter. If someone can no longer manage their own affairs and has no power of attorney in place, a family member may have to ask a court to appoint a guardian or conservator.¹ What applies to your parent depends on their state and their documents, so questions about any of this belong with an elder law attorney.

## Finding the paperwork

Most of the seven areas depend on paperwork, and it's usually scattered. The records most families need first are the medical ones: insurance cards, a current medication list, doctor and specialist contacts, and anything from a recent hospital stay, including **discharge papers, treatment or therapy schedules, and rehabilitation or home-exercise instructions**. Chapter 7 covers the full set of important documents and how to keep track of where each one lives. The National Institute on Aging also publishes a free checklist of personal, financial, health, and legal records that's a useful companion.²

## Gathering information without taking over

It's easy, once you start, to slide from helping your parent get organized into running their life for them. Most parents notice that shift right away, and it can shut the whole effort down.

Ask where, not what. "Where do you keep your insurance cards?" is easier to answer than "What insurance do you have?" and it keeps your parent in charge of their own things. Borrow a reason when it helps: "I'm updating my own paperwork and realized I don't know where yours is" lands very differently from "We need to talk about your documents."

Notice what's in plain sight, such as the mail pile, the pill bottles on the counter, or the discharge folder on the table, and write it down later. Going through drawers without permission is a different thing, and it costs trust you'll need. Keep it to one or two questions per visit; small asks feel like help, and a list of twenty feels like an audit. Offering to drive to an appointment and asking if you can sit in is often the easiest way to learn who the doctors are.

If your parent doesn't want to share something, that's their right. You can come back to it.

One note on doctors: they may talk with you, but they don't have to. Federal privacy rules don't require providers to share information with family unless that person is the patient's legal personal representative, though providers may share relevant information with family involved in a patient's care if the patient agrees or doesn't object.³ Chapter 5 explains how to set up permission properly. In the meantime, the rules limit what doctors can tell you, not what you can tell them.

## How one family did it

*The Lindqvist family is a composite created for this book. They aren't real people, but their situation is a common one.*

Grace Lindqvist is 76, a retired school librarian who lives alone and walks her dog every morning. In September she went to the emergency room with shortness of breath, spent four days in the hospital, and came home with a new heart diagnosis, a procedure behind her, five new or changed medications, a ten-pound lifting limit, a referral to cardiac rehab three mornings a week, and a folder of papers she hadn't read yet.

Her son, Ben, lives fifteen minutes away. His first instinct was to take everything over. He'd already started a list titled "Mom's heart plan" when it occurred to him that his mother had been managing her own life for fifty years and hadn't asked him to manage it now.

So he started smaller. He asked, "Would it help if I made copies of the hospital papers, so we both have them?" She said yes. He didn't read through them in front of her. Later, he wrote down the diagnosis exactly as the papers stated it, the hospital and dates, the procedure, the names of the cardiologist and the rehab program, the lifting limit, and the medication changes.

He asked his mother what the cardiologist had told her, rather than guessing from the papers, and whether she'd like company at the follow-up visit. She did, and at that visit she told the doctor it was fine to talk with Ben. When he asked about access to her patient portal, she said she'd rather not yet. He let it go.

The rehab schedule turned out to be the real problem. Grace wasn't allowed to drive for a few weeks, and the sessions were across town at 8 a.m. Ben couldn't do all of them. His sister Nora, two states away, couldn't drive her either, but she could make phone calls. She found that Grace's church had a volunteer driver program and set up two of the three weekly rides.

Ben also wrote down something no paperwork would tell him: what his mother was like before September. Walked the dog a mile and a half every morning. Sharp as a tack. Ate a real breakfast. Three weeks later, when Grace mentioned she was "too tired" to walk the dog at all, Ben had something to compare it to, and Grace brought it up at her next appointment.

Grace still runs her own life. Ben and Nora just know a lot more about it than they did in August.

## Ranking what matters most

Once you've worked through the seven areas, even partially, rate each one: fine for now, needs a plan this month, or needs action within weeks. Then pick no more than three to focus on.

Three questions help you choose. Could this cause serious harm soon? Will it be hard to undo if it goes wrong? Is there a deadline, such as a restriction that ends, a follow-up that hasn't been booked, or legal documents your parent may want to deal with while they can fully take part? Those three become the core of your first-month plan, which Chapter 10 helps you build. Everything else gets written down and waits its turn.

## A few things you might say

"Mom, I'm getting my own paperwork together and realized I don't know where yours is. You don't have to show me anything. Could you just tell me where you keep things, so I'd know where to look if you were ever in the hospital?"

"Would it help if I made a copy of the discharge papers so we both have them? That way, if someone calls me, I'm not guessing."

And if the answer is "That's private": "That's fair, and I won't push. Could we at least agree on one person who knows where everything is, in case of an emergency? It could be me, your sister, or your attorney."

## Who can help fill in the gaps

Your parent's primary care doctor is a good place to bring the overall picture. If your parent has Medicare Part B, the yearly "wellness" visit is meant for building a prevention plan and includes a check for signs of cognitive problems. Medicare covers it once every 12 months, and your parent pays nothing if the provider accepts assignment, though other tests or services during the same visit may cost extra. It isn't a routine physical exam.⁴

A pharmacist can review every medication and supplement with your parent, which is especially useful after a hospital stay. And if legal documents are missing or out of date, an elder law attorney in your parent's state is the right person to ask. Chapter 9 covers other kinds of outside help.

## Where to start
