# Chapter 3

# Everyone Says "Get Organized," But Organize What?

Someone tells you that you need to get organized. A website says to "gather important documents." So you buy a binder, write your father's name on the spine, and sit down with it. Then nothing happens, because you don't actually know what goes in it. Which documents? Where would they even be? Does "important" include the cable bill? The binder sits empty on the counter for three weeks, and now you feel behind on something you can't even describe.

The advice isn't wrong. It just stops before it gets useful. This chapter fills in the rest: seven specific areas of your parent's life, what's worth knowing in each one, where the paperwork usually lives, and how to collect it without turning every visit into an interview.

## What you're actually building

Generic advice to "get organized" fails in a few predictable ways. It doesn't say what to collect. It treats every gap as equally urgent. And it assumes you can simply ask your parent for everything, when in reality they may not know where things are, may be embarrassed about it, or may hear "Where's your will?" as "I'm planning your funeral."

It helps to know that you're building two things.

The first is a picture. That means the basic facts (names, phone numbers, documents, appointments) plus a realistic sense of how your parent is doing day to day. Professionals need both. "Dad's on a lot of pills" isn't much to work with. "Dad takes nine medications from two pharmacies, and two of them changed when he left the hospital" is.

The second is a ranking. You won't sort out seven areas of someone's life in a month, and you don't need to. You need to know which few gaps could cause the most trouble soonest, so you can deal with those first and let the rest wait.

A note on tone before we start. None of this is meant to become a medical chart. You don't need medical vocabulary, and you don't need to understand every diagnosis. Write things down the way you'd explain them to a friend, and copy exact names and numbers straight off the paperwork. If your notes would make sense to a sibling reading them at a hospital desk, they're doing their job.

## The seven areas

### 1. Health and medical care

This is usually the biggest section, and it's the one families most often find half-filled when they need it. You're trying to capture what's going on medically and who's involved, not to understand the medicine.

**Current diagnoses.** The conditions your parent is being treated for, in the words their doctors use. Copy them from a visit summary or discharge paper rather than relying on memory. "Something with his kidneys" is a start; the exact name is better.

**The most recent hospital stay.** When it was, which hospital, and the reason, as the hospital described it. If there's been more than one in the past year, note each.

**Recent surgery or procedures.** What was done, when, and where. This matters for follow-up appointments and for any doctor who sees your parent next.

**The doctors involved.** The primary care doctor and every specialist, with what each one treats and how to reach them. Many families discover they have no idea who the cardiologist is until someone asks.

**Treatment or therapy schedules.** Anything that happens on a regular calendar: dialysis, infusions, injections, wound care visits, cardiac or pulmonary rehab, physical therapy sessions, regular lab work. Write down the days, times, and place.

**Discharge instructions.** If your parent has been in the hospital or had a procedure recently, the discharge papers often say what to watch for, what to avoid, which appointments to make, and who to call. Keep a copy where you can find it.

**Activity or lifting restrictions.** "No lifting more than ten pounds." "No driving for two weeks." "Keep the leg elevated." These are easy to forget once someone feels better, so write them down with the date they end, if there is one.

**Therapy or home health involvement.** Whether a physical therapist, occupational therapist, or home health agency is involved, who they are, how often they come, and how to reach them.

**Insurance.** Which plans your parent has (Medicare, a supplement or Medicare Advantage plan, a drug plan, any other coverage) and where the cards are. A photo of the front and back of each card is a good start.

### 2. Medications

List every prescription, over-the-counter medicine, vitamin, and supplement, with the dose and how often it's taken. Note which pharmacy fills each prescription, because a parent using more than one pharmacy is common and worth knowing about.

Pay special attention to **recent medication changes**: anything new, stopped, or changed in dose in the past few months, and especially after a hospital stay. Write down what changed, when, and who changed it. You're not evaluating whether the change was right. You're making sure everyone involved is working from the same list.

The simplest way to build the list is often to gather every bottle in one place, including the ones in the bathroom drawer and the car, and take a photo of each label.

### 3. Daily functioning and physical condition

This section is about how your parent is actually managing, which is often more useful to professionals than any list of diagnoses.

**Their normal baseline.** Start here. How does your parent usually move, think, eat, and get through the day when things are going well? "Walks the dog a mile every morning." "Does the crossword in pen." "Cooks dinner most nights." "Gets a little foggy in the late evening." This is the yardstick you'll measure every future change against, and it's the thing hospital and clinic staff most often wish families could tell them.

**Mobility changes.** Walking more slowly, holding onto furniture, trouble with stairs or getting up from a chair, a recent fall, using a cane they didn't use before.

**Pain, fatigue, appetite, and hydration.** Is your parent in more pain than usual? More tired? Eating less, skipping meals, or drinking very little? These are easy to overlook, and they often matter. If they're new or sudden, Chapter 2 helps you decide how quickly to call someone.

**Everyday tasks.** Professionals often think about two groups of activities. The first is basic self-care: eating, bathing, dressing, using the toilet, getting in and out of bed or a chair. The second is the work of running a life: cooking, shopping, housekeeping, managing medications, handling money, using the phone, and getting around. For each, the simple question is whether your parent does it independently, with some help, or not at all right now.

**Durable medical equipment.** Walkers, canes, wheelchairs, shower chairs, raised toilet seats, hospital beds, oxygen, CPAP machines, blood pressure cuffs, glucose meters. Note what your parent has, what was ordered but hasn't arrived, and what they've been told they need but don't have yet. Also note who supplies it, since that's who you'll call when something breaks or runs out.

### 4. Home safety

Walk through the house the way a first-time visitor would, at the time of day your parent is most likely to be up and moving around in dim light. Loose rugs, clutter on the floor, stairs without sturdy railings, a dark path from the bed to the bathroom, a bathtub without grab bars, smoke detectors that haven't been tested in years, and a phone that can't be reached from the floor if someone falls. You're not redesigning the house. You're writing down what you see. (Home safety and independence get a full book later in this series.)

### 5. Transportation

Two questions here. First, is your parent still driving, and how is it going? New dents, tickets, getting lost on familiar routes, or friends who've quietly stopped accepting rides are all worth noting. Second, and just as important, how does your parent get where they need to go, especially to medical appointments? If they have a regular treatment or therapy schedule, someone has to get them there. Note who does it now, what happens if that person can't, and whether a driving restriction from a doctor (after surgery, for example) has left a gap.

### 6. Finances

You're looking for the outline, not the details: where income comes from, which bills are regular, which accounts exist and where the statements go, and whether anything is set up on autopay. Watch for unopened mail, past-due notices, or unusual withdrawals or "gifts." Who handles money, and whether anyone else should know how, is a conversation that deserves care, and later chapters return to it.

### 7. Legal documents

Find out whether your parent has a health care power of attorney (sometimes called a health care proxy), a financial power of attorney, a living will, and a will, and where the signed originals are kept. These are separate documents that do different jobs. A health care power of attorney names someone to make medical decisions if your parent can't. A financial power of attorney names someone to handle money and property.

This area runs on a clock the others don't. Documents like these generally need to be signed while your parent can understand what they're signing, and the rules depend on your state. If someone can no longer manage their own affairs and there's no power of attorney in place, a family member may have to go to court to be appointed as a guardian or conservator.¹ That process takes time and money, and it's avoidable for many families who plan ahead. That's why legal paperwork deserves early attention even when everything else looks calm. An elder law attorney in your parent's state can explain what applies to them.

## Records you may need to find

Most of the seven areas depend on paperwork, and the paperwork is usually scattered. Here's what to look for. You don't need all of it this week; knowing where each item lives is half the job.

- Insurance cards (front and back) for every plan
- A current medication list, or the bottles themselves
- Doctor and specialist names and numbers
- Hospital discharge papers from any recent stay
- Treatment or therapy schedules (rehab, dialysis, infusions, wound care, physical therapy)
- Rehabilitation or home-exercise instructions from a physical or occupational therapist
- Recent visit summaries or test results your parent has been given
- Contact information for any home health agency or equipment supplier
- Health care and financial powers of attorney, living will, and will
- Recent bank statements and a list of regular bills
- Social Security and pension information
- Car title, registration, and auto insurance

The National Institute on Aging publishes a free checklist of personal, financial, health, and legal records worth gathering, which is a good companion to this list.²

## Gathering information without taking over

It's easy, once you start, to slide from helping your parent get organized into running their life for them. Most parents will notice that shift right away, and it can shut the whole effort down. A few approaches tend to work better:

Ask where, not what. "Where do you keep your insurance cards?" is easier to answer than "What insurance do you have?" It also keeps your parent in charge of their own things.

Borrow a reason. "I'm updating my own paperwork and realized I don't know where yours is" lands very differently from "We need to talk about your documents."

Look, don't search. Notice what's in plain sight (the mail pile, the pill bottles on the counter, the walker in the corner, the discharge folder on the table) and write it down later. Going through drawers without permission is a different thing, and it costs trust you'll need later.

One or two questions per visit. Small asks feel like help. A list of twenty feels like an audit.

Ask to come along. Offering to drive to an appointment, and asking if you can sit in, is often the easiest way to learn who the doctors are and what they've said.

Respect "not yet." If your parent doesn't want to share something, that's their right. You can come back to it.

### Talking with your parent's doctors

You may assume doctors will automatically talk with you because you're family. They might, but they don't have to. According to the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services, the federal health privacy rule (HIPAA) doesn't require providers to share information with family or friends unless that person is the patient's legal personal representative. Providers may share relevant information with family members involved in a patient's care if the patient agrees or doesn't object.³

In practice, the smoothest path is usually for your parent to tell their doctors, in writing if the office has a form for it, that it's okay to talk with you. You can also ask whether the patient portal allows a family member to have their own access. Either way, it's your parent's decision.

And remember that privacy rules limit what doctors can tell you, not what you can tell them. You can always share what you've observed.

## How one family did it

*The Lindqvist family is a composite created for this book. They aren't real people, but their situation is a common one.*

Grace Lindqvist is 76, a retired school librarian who lives alone and walks her dog every morning. In September she went to the emergency room with shortness of breath, spent four days in the hospital, and came home with a new heart diagnosis, a procedure behind her, five new or changed medications, a ten-pound lifting limit, a referral to cardiac rehab three mornings a week, and a folder of papers she hadn't read yet.

Her son, Ben, lives fifteen minutes away. His first instinct was to take everything over: call the cardiologist, set up her pills, rearrange her week. He'd already started a list titled "Mom's heart plan" when it occurred to him that his mother had been managing her own life for fifty years and hadn't asked him to manage it now.

So he started smaller. On his first visit he asked, "Would it help if I made copies of the hospital papers, so we both have them?" She said yes. He didn't read through them in front of her. He copied them, and later wrote down the diagnosis exactly as the papers stated it, the hospital and dates, the procedure, the names of the cardiologist and the rehab program, the lifting limit, and the medication changes.

He asked his mother what the cardiologist had told her, rather than guessing from the papers. He asked whether she'd like company at the follow-up visit. She did, and at that visit she told the doctor it was fine to talk with Ben. When he asked about getting access to her patient portal, she said she'd rather not yet. He let it go.

The rehab schedule turned out to be the real problem. Her doctor had told her not to drive for a few weeks, and the sessions were across town at 8 a.m. Ben couldn't do all of them. His sister Nora, who lives two states away, couldn't drive her, but she could make phone calls. She found out that Grace's church had a volunteer driver program and set up two of the three weekly rides.

Ben also wrote down something no paperwork would tell him: what his mother was like before September. Walked the dog a mile and a half every morning. Sharp as a tack. Ate a real breakfast. Three weeks later, when Grace mentioned she was "too tired" to walk the dog at all, Ben had something to compare it to, and Grace brought it up at her next appointment.

Grace still runs her own life. Ben and Nora just know a lot more about it than they did in August.

## Ranking what matters most

Once you've worked through the seven areas, even partially, rate each one:

- Fine for now: nothing urgent, check again in a few months.
- Needs a plan this month: a real gap, but not dangerous in the next few weeks.
- Act within weeks: a gap that could cause harm soon or that will be hard to fix later.

Then pick no more than three to focus on. Three questions help: Could this cause serious harm soon? Will it be hard to undo if it goes wrong? Is there a deadline, such as a restriction that ends, a follow-up appointment that hasn't been booked, or legal documents that need to be signed while your parent can still take part? Those three become the core of your first-month plan. Everything else gets written down and waits its turn.

## What to say

**About papers:** "Mom, I'm getting my own paperwork together and realized I don't know where yours is. You don't have to show me anything. Could you just tell me where you keep things, so I'd know where to look if you were ever in the hospital?"

**About hospital paperwork:** "Would it help if I made a copy of the discharge papers so we both have them? That way, if someone calls me, I'm not guessing."

**About doctors:** "Would you be okay telling Dr. Patel it's all right to talk with me? You'd still make every decision. I'd just stop having to guess."

**About medications:** "Would you be willing to put every bottle and supplement in a bag and have the pharmacist look them over with us? I'd like to make sure the list is right after all the changes."

**When they say "That's private":** "That's fair, and I won't push. Could we at least agree on one person who knows where everything is, in case of an emergency? It could be me, your sister, or your attorney."

**To a professional:** "I've put together a short summary of what we've noticed and what's changed. I'm not asking you to diagnose anything. I'd like to know what you think we should pay attention to first."

## When to bring in professional help

- Your parent's primary care doctor. A good place to bring the overall picture. If your parent has Medicare Part B, the yearly "wellness" visit is designed for building a prevention plan and includes a check for signs of cognitive problems. Medicare covers it once every 12 months, and your parent pays nothing if the provider accepts assignment, although other tests or services during the same visit may cost extra. It isn't a routine physical exam.⁴
- A pharmacist. Ask for a review of every medication and supplement, especially after a hospital stay or any recent change.
- The discharge planner, therapist, or home health agency. If your parent was recently discharged, these are the people to call with questions about instructions, equipment, or therapy schedules.
- An elder law attorney. If legal documents are missing or out of date, especially if there are any concerns about memory, sooner is better. Laws vary by state.
- Your parent's bank or brokerage firm. Your parent can name a "trusted contact," someone the firm can reach if it can't get in touch with your parent or suspects financial exploitation. According to FINRA, a trusted contact at a brokerage firm has no authority to make decisions or transactions on the account.⁵ Banks may have their own versions; ask what they offer.
