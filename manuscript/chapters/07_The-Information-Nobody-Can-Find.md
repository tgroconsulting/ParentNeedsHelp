# Chapter 7

# The Information Nobody Can Find

## Getting the Essentials Organized

It's Saturday afternoon and your father's neighbor is on the phone. Dad's been taken to the emergency room after feeling dizzy, he's fine for now, and the nurse wants to know who his heart doctor is. You don't know. Your brother thinks it starts with an M. The pharmacy might be the one by the grocery store, or maybe the one Dad switched to last year. Somebody has a key to the house, but nobody can remember who. Meanwhile the nurse is waiting.

None of this is a crisis on its own. Every piece of it can eventually be found. But each missing piece costs time, and it always seems to go missing at exactly the moment someone needs it.

The fix isn't complicated. It's a simple, central place where the essentials live, kept by someone who updates it, and known to the people who might need it. This chapter shows you how to build one without turning it into a filing project or collecting more of your parent's private information than you need.

## What missing information actually costs

It's easy to think of disorganization as a minor inconvenience. In practice, it creates the kinds of delays and mix-ups families most want to avoid.

A hospital or emergency room asks for a medication list, the name of a specialist, or insurance information, and nobody has it. A prescription runs out while your parent is staying with you, and you can't remember which pharmacy has it on file. A home health agency calls with a schedule change and reaches someone who doesn't know what it's about. A sibling who covers for you for a week spends half of it figuring out who to call. A follow-up appointment the doctor wanted never gets made, because nobody wrote it down.

None of these is anyone's fault. Information that lived comfortably in one person's head, usually your parent's, now has to be findable by other people, sometimes in a hurry. That's the whole job of this chapter.

## What the system is

If you worked through the earlier chapters, you've already started. The Seven-Area Assessment in Chapter 3 helped you figure out what exists and where. The appointment worksheet in Chapter 5 helped you track individual visits. This chapter pulls the most important pieces into one place that's easy to keep current.

The system has four parts:

1. **Contacts:** who to call, for what.
2. **A running list** of appointments, follow-ups, and open questions.
3. **Document locations:** where important papers are kept, not copies of all of them.
4. **Current responsibilities:** who in the family is handling what.

It can live on paper, in a password-protected document, or both. What matters more than the format is that there's one version, someone keeps it current, and the right people know where it is.

## The contacts worth having

Start with the people and organizations someone is most likely to need in a hurry, then fill in the rest over time.

**Your parent's basics.** Full legal name, date of birth, address, and the names of their insurance plans (not the full member numbers; more on that below).

**Emergency contacts.** Who to call first, second, and third, with phone numbers. Note who lives closest and who has a key to the house.

**Health care providers.** The primary care doctor and every specialist, with what each one treats. "Dr. Morales, cardiology, (555) 555-0142" is far more useful than a name alone. Include the after-hours number if there is one.

**Pharmacies.** Every pharmacy your parent uses, including mail-order. Many people use more than one without anyone else realizing it.

**Hospital or emergency preference.** If your parent has a hospital they prefer, or one where their doctors practice, note it.

**Other care involved.** Home health agencies, physical or occupational therapists, equipment suppliers, a meal delivery program, a transportation service.

**Insurance and benefits contacts.** Customer service numbers for each health and drug plan, usually printed on the back of the card.

**Community and service organizations.** Your parent's Area Agency on Aging, faith community, senior center, volunteer drivers, or anyone else who provides regular help.

**Neighbors and friends.** The people who see your parent most often and could check in if needed, with your parent's okay.

**Other professionals.** If your parent works with an attorney, accountant, or financial advisor, their names and contact information belong here. You don't need to know what those professionals do for your parent. You just need to know how to reach them.

**The house itself.** Landlord or property manager, the plumber or handyman they trust, utility companies. And if there's a pet, the vet and whoever could care for it in an emergency.

## One running list

The second part of the system is a single list of things that are coming up or still unresolved. This is where the scattered sticky notes and half-remembered phone calls go.

Keep it simple. Each item needs four things: what it is, who's handling it, when it's due, and whether it's done.

| Added | Item | Who | Due | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Oct. 3 | Cardiology follow-up | Dad schedules, Kim drives | Within 6 weeks | Booked Nov. 14 |
| Oct. 3 | Blood work ordered | Kim | Before Nov. 14 | Not yet |
| Oct. 10 | Ask pharmacist about two similar pills | Dad and Kim | Next refill | Open |
| Oct. 12 | Walker from supplier | Supplier calls Dad | ? | Waiting |

Appointments can go on a calendar as well, as Chapter 5 suggested. The running list is for everything that falls between appointments: the follow-ups, callbacks, questions, and errands that tend to disappear.

Once a week or so, look at the list. Cross off what's done, move anything that's stuck, and notice items that have been "waiting" for a while. That last group is where most dropped balls are hiding.

## Collect less than you think

When families start organizing, there's a strong pull to gather everything: copies of every card, every account number, every statement. More feels safer. It usually isn't.

The more sensitive information you collect, the more harm it can do if a phone is lost, a document is shared with the wrong person, or an email account is hacked. And most of the time, what you really need is to know where something is and who to call, not to have a copy of it in your own files.

A few guidelines:

- **Record locations, not contents, wherever you can.** "Bank statements: top drawer of the desk in the den" is enough for most purposes.
- **Don't put full account numbers, passwords, or PINs on the organizer.** If those ever need to be shared, that's your parent's decision, and they should be stored separately and securely.
- **Be careful with identifying numbers.** The Social Security Administration advises keeping a Social Security card in a safe place at home, not carrying it, and not giving out the number unnecessarily.¹ Medicare cards use a Medicare number rather than a Social Security number, and Medicare advises protecting that number like a credit card and sharing it only with providers, insurers, and trusted people who work with Medicare.²
- **Insurance card photos are useful but sensitive.** If you keep them, store them where only the people who need them can see them, not in a shared photo album or a group text.
- **Clear out what's outdated.** Old medication lists, expired cards, and lists of doctors your parent no longer sees create confusion. The Federal Trade Commission includes prescription information for medicines you no longer take on its list of papers to shred.³

## Your parent's information, your parent's call

Everything on this organizer is about your parent. It belongs to them, and they should decide what's on it and who sees it.

That means building it with your parent rather than about them wherever possible. Many parents are glad to have it once they understand the purpose: "If you're ever in the hospital, I want whoever's there to be able to answer the nurse's questions." Some will want to keep the master copy themselves. That's a perfectly good arrangement, as long as someone else knows where it is.

It also helps to think about levels of access:

- **An emergency page** that anyone who might be with your parent in a crisis can see. This is a single sheet with the basics: name, date of birth, emergency contacts, primary doctor, pharmacy, where the medication list is, and insurance plan names. Some families keep a copy on the fridge or in the parent's wallet, and one in the phones of the closest family members.
- **The full organizer,** shared with your parent and one or two people who are actively helping.
- **Anything more sensitive,** shared only as your parent directs.

For paper, a clearly labeled folder kept in a consistent place at your parent's home works well. For digital copies, use a password-protected file or account, share it with specific people rather than through an open link, and use strong, unique passwords, as the Social Security Administration recommends for any account holding personal information.¹ Avoid keeping the organizer in a group chat, where it's easy to forward and impossible to update.

## Documents: where they are, not what they mean

Part of the organizer is a list of important documents and where each one is kept. The Federal Trade Commission's list of papers worth locking up and keeping includes birth certificates, Social Security cards, passports, marriage and divorce papers, military records, wills, living wills, powers of attorney, and retirement and pension records.³ Your parent may also have property deeds, vehicle titles, insurance policies, and recent tax returns.

For each document, the organizer only needs a few things: whether it exists, where the original is kept, whether anyone else has a copy, and who to contact about it (the attorney who prepared it, the bank holding a safe deposit box, the insurance company).

That's deliberately limited. Knowing that a power of attorney exists and where it's kept is very different from knowing what it allows, whether it's in effect, or who can act under it. Those are questions for the attorney who prepared it, or for the bank, hospital, or other institution being asked to rely on it. Don't try to answer them yourself, and don't write your own interpretation on the organizer. If a document is missing or seems out of date, note that as an open question for your parent to raise with the appropriate professional if they choose to.

## Who's handling what

The last part of the system is a short list of current responsibilities. In many families, roles develop by accident: one sibling started driving to appointments and never stopped, another handles the insurance calls because she did it once. That works until someone assumes someone else is doing something, and nobody is.

Write down the regular jobs and who does each one: picking up prescriptions, scheduling appointments, going to appointments, paying bills (if your parent has asked for help with that), the weekly check-in call, yard work, the backup person for each. Include your parent, who is probably still doing most of it. Keep it factual. This is a record of how things work now, not a judgment about who's doing enough. (How families divide this work, and what to do when it feels uneven, gets a book of its own later in this series.)

## Keeping it current

An out-of-date organizer can be worse than none at all, because people trust it. A few habits keep it accurate:

- **Put a "last updated" date at the top of every page.**
- **Update after anything changes:** an appointment, a hospital stay, a new medication or provider, a change in insurance, a move, or a change in who's handling what.
- **Pick one keeper.** One person is responsible for making updates, even if several people send them information. This avoids two versions drifting apart.
- **Do a quick monthly review.** Ten minutes: are the phone numbers right, is the running list current, has anything changed?
- **Do a fuller review every few months,** ideally with your parent. Remove outdated information, confirm document locations, and check that the right people have the current version.
- **Replace old copies when you update.** Swap the sheet on the fridge, re-send the file, and ask people to discard the old version.

## How it helps

Here's a made-up example of what this looks like when it works.

Six weeks after Kim builds her father's organizer, he's taken to the emergency room after a fall. His neighbor, who has a copy of the emergency page, rides along and hands it to the nurse: medications, cardiologist, pharmacy, insurance plans, and Kim's number. Kim's brother, who lives out of state, pulls up the full organizer on his phone and calls the cardiologist's office while Kim drives to the hospital. Nobody had to guess, and nobody had to search the house for a folder.

It doesn't make the day less stressful. It just removes one layer of it.
