# Chapter 2

# Is This an Emergency or Just a Bad Week?

It's 8:40 on a Sunday night. Your mother's cough sounds worse than it did on Friday. Your father mentions, almost in passing, that he's been "a little dizzy" since he started the new blood pressure pill. A neighbor texts that Dad looked wobbly at the mailbox. Your sister wants to know if somebody should drive over. And all of it is arriving at the same volume.

When everything feels equally urgent, people tend to do one of two things. They rush to the emergency room over something that could have waited for a phone call on Monday, or they decide it's probably nothing, go to bed, and lie awake. Both are understandable. Both come from having worries but no way to sort them.

This chapter gives you a way to sort them. It won't turn you into a nurse, and it isn't meant to. Your job in these moments is narrower than that: notice that something has changed, decide how quickly someone qualified needs to hear about it, and get it to the right person. The three-color system below is built for exactly that.

## Why sorting is harder than it sounds

Part of the difficulty is that you may not have a clear sense of your parent's normal yet. If you don't know how steady they usually are on their feet, how sharp they usually are in the evening, or how much they usually eat, every change looks equally alarming. The observations you started collecting in Chapter 1 help here more than you might expect.

The other part is that serious problems in older adults don't always look serious. The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute notes that heart attack symptoms can start slowly and be mild, that shortness of breath is a more common symptom in older adults, and that "silent" heart attacks with very mild or no symptoms are more common in older adults and people with diabetes.¹ Sudden confusion can be a sign that something physical is going on; MedlinePlus lists infections such as urinary tract infections and pneumonia, and certain medicines, among the causes of delirium.² A parent who "just isn't themselves today" may have a perfectly ordinary bad day, or may need to be seen.

The reverse is true too. Most bad weeks really are just bad weeks. The point of a system isn't to make you suspicious of everything. It's to help you act quickly on the few things that can't wait, so the rest can go back to being ordinary.

You don't need to figure out what's causing a change. You need to recognize the change and get it to someone who can.

## Three questions

Before you reach for a color, ask yourself three things:

1. **Is this sudden?** Did it start in the last few hours or days, or has it been building for months?
2. **Could it cause serious harm in the next few hours?** Not "is it bad," but "could waiting make it much worse?"
3. **Is it different from my parent's normal?** A slow walker who's walking slowly is one thing. A brisk walker who suddenly can't stand is another.

Sudden, potentially serious, and different from normal points toward red. Gradual, stable, and familiar points toward green. Most things land somewhere in between, which is what yellow is for.

## RED: Call 911 now

Red means your parent may need emergency care, and the right move is to call 911 rather than drive them yourself or wait to see if it passes. Some examples:

**Trouble breathing, or chest pain or pressure.** The National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute advises calling 911 for possible heart attack symptoms even if you aren't sure that's what it is, and not driving yourself or letting someone else drive, because emergency medical services can start treatment on the way.¹ Remember that in an older adult, the main sign may be shortness of breath rather than chest pain.

**Signs of a possible stroke.** Sudden numbness or weakness of the face, arm, or leg (especially on one side), sudden confusion or trouble speaking or understanding, sudden trouble seeing, sudden trouble walking or loss of balance, or a sudden severe headache with no known cause. The National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke suggests a quick check: Face (does one side droop when they smile?), Arms (does one drift down when both are raised?), Speech (is it slurred or strange?), and Time (call 911 right away). Note the time the symptoms started.³

**Sudden confusion, or a parent who isn't alert.** Confusion that comes on suddenly, a parent who is hard to wake or keeps drifting off mid-sentence, or anyone who loses consciousness, even briefly. MedlinePlus advises calling 911 when confusion comes on suddenly, when it's the first time someone has become confused for no apparent reason, when it follows a head injury, or when the person becomes unconscious.⁴

**A serious fall.** A fall where your parent can't get up, is in severe pain, can't move a limb normally, or hit their head and is now acting differently: confused, unusually sleepy, weak, or having a seizure.

**Signs of a serious infection.** An illness or infection along with confusion, shortness of breath, a fast heartbeat or weak pulse, clammy or sweaty skin, shivering or feeling very cold, or extreme pain. The CDC lists these as possible signs of sepsis and describes sepsis as a medical emergency.⁵ You don't need to know whether it's sepsis. The combination is enough to call.

**Severe dehydration.** Not drinking, or unable to keep fluids down, along with confusion, dizziness, very little or no urine, or a racing heart or breathing. MedlinePlus advises calling 911 if a dehydrated person has a change in alertness, such as confusion, or loses consciousness, or if they aren't improving or are getting worse despite treatment.⁶

**A possible medication overdose with serious symptoms.** If your parent took too much of something, or the wrong thing, and is now hard to wake, having trouble breathing, or having a seizure, call 911.

**Danger to themselves or someone else.** Including any talk of suicide with a plan or the means to carry it out.

## YELLOW: Call a clinician today

Yellow means something has changed and someone qualified should hear about it today, but there are no red flags right now. Usually that's a call to your parent's doctor's office, the after-hours line, a pharmacist, or a nurse advice line. They may tell you it can wait, they may want to see your parent today, or they may send you to urgent care or the emergency room. Your job is to make the call and describe what you've seen.

**A change in thinking or alertness that's new but not severe.** Your parent is more muddled than usual since yesterday, or seems "off" in a way that's different from their normal forgetfulness, but they're awake, talking, and none of the red signs are present. MedlinePlus advises contacting a provider when there's a rapid change in mental status.²

**Any fall, even without an obvious injury.** A doctor may want to check for injury or look into why it happened. If your parent takes a blood thinner, a serious fall or any hit to the head is worth a prompt call to their provider; MedlinePlus advises people taking warfarin to contact their provider in exactly that situation.⁷ Head injuries in older adults can also cause problems that show up later. MedlinePlus advises contacting a provider right away if confusion, weakness, or numbness appears weeks or even months after a head injury in an older adult.⁸ If you notice a change like that, mention the fall, however long ago it was.

**A medication mistake.** A double dose, a skipped dose of something important, pills taken from the wrong bottle, someone else's prescription. If there are no red-flag symptoms, call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222. According to the Health Resources and Services Administration, the line is free and confidential, open 24 hours a day, and answered by a nurse, pharmacist, or doctor at your local poison center, and people usually get the help they need over the phone.⁹ Your parent's pharmacist is another good call for questions about what was taken.

**A possible reaction to a medication.** New dizziness, drowsiness, confusion, stomach trouble, a rash, or anything else that started around the time a medicine was added or changed. Call the prescriber or pharmacist and tell them what you've noticed and when it started. Don't stop, skip, or change a medication on your own; that decision belongs to the prescriber.

**Not eating or drinking well.** A day or more of eating or drinking much less than usual, or early signs of dehydration such as thirst, dry mouth, darker urine, or urinating less. MedlinePlus notes that older adults are at higher risk for dehydration and advises calling a provider if you think someone may become dehydrated, before it gets serious.⁶

**Symptoms that are getting worse, or not getting better as expected.** A cough that's worsening instead of easing, pain that's climbing, a wound that's becoming redder, warmer, or more painful, a parent who was recovering and has started going backward. If something is worsening fast, or the red signs appear, it moves to red.

**Changes after surgery or a hospital stay.** More on this below.

**Hopelessness, or talk of not wanting to be here.** If there's no immediate danger, you can call, text, or chat with the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline. It's available around the clock, free and confidential, and it's there for people worried about a loved one as well as for the person who's struggling.¹⁰ If there is a plan or immediate danger, that's red: call 911.

## GREEN: Log it and plan for it

Green is for changes that matter but aren't urgent. Forgetfulness that has crept in over months. A house that's gotten harder to keep up. Hearing or vision that's slowly declining. A single missed appointment. More time alone than before. Bills falling behind. No legal documents in place.

Green doesn't mean ignore. It means write it down, using the habits from Chapter 1, and bring it up at your parent's next appointment or in the planning you'll do over the coming weeks. Most of the rest of this book is about green items. Green is also where you go back and look for patterns: one yellow in a year is an incident, but three in a month is something to raise with your parent's doctor.

## The tiebreaker

When you can't decide between two colors, go one color up.

If you're torn between green and yellow, make the call. If you're torn between yellow and red, call 911. Being wrong in that direction usually costs you a slightly awkward phone call or a long evening in a waiting room. Being wrong in the other direction can cost a great deal more. Nobody on the other end of the line will think less of you for checking.

## After surgery or a hospital stay

The days and weeks after a hospital stay or surgery deserve their own mention, because a lot can change quickly and families are often still getting their bearings. Your parent may come home on new medications, with new limits on activity, and with instructions that were explained once, quickly, at discharge. MedlinePlus notes that delirium can follow surgery and general anesthesia and is common in intensive care, especially in older adults.²

Discharge paperwork usually tells you who to call with questions, problems, or an emergency. MedlinePlus includes that on its list of what a discharge plan should cover.¹¹ Find that page and keep it near the phone.

If your parent was recently discharged and has instructions, use the hospital's or clinician's after-hours number when the instructions say to, or when something worries you but isn't a red flag. Those numbers exist so families can reach someone who knows the recent hospital stay. For red-flag symptoms, don't wait for a callback: call 911.

Hospital discharge and care transitions get their own book in this series. For now, the point is simply that "recently out of the hospital" should nudge you toward making the call.

## How the colors play out

Here's a made-up example to show how the same worry can land in three different places.

A neighbor calls to say your father seemed confused when she stopped by and kept asking what day it was.

- If you know he's been mixing up days for most of the past year, and this sounds like more of the same, it's **green**. Write it down, note the neighbor noticed, and add it to the list for his next appointment.
- If he was fine on the phone with you two days ago and this is new, it's **yellow**. Call his doctor's office today, or the after-hours line if it's evening, and describe exactly what changed and when.
- If he's also slurring his words, or can't lift one arm, or the neighbor can barely keep him awake, it's **red**. Call 911.

Notice that in none of these cases are you deciding what's wrong with him. You're deciding how fast someone qualified needs to know.

## What to say

When you call, have a few facts ready: your parent's name and date of birth, what changed and when it started, what's normal for them, what medications they take (or where the list is), and any recent hospital stay or surgery. A few scripts you can adapt:

**To a 911 dispatcher:** "My father is 82. Since about 2:15 this afternoon his speech has been slurred and his left arm is weak. He takes a blood thinner. We're at [address]."

**To the doctor's office or after-hours line:** "I'm calling about my mother, [name, date of birth]. Since yesterday she's been more confused than usual. That's a sudden change for her. She takes [medications] and was discharged from [hospital] on [date]. I'd like someone to advise us today. What should we do?"

**To Poison Help or a pharmacist:** "My mother is 79. About an hour ago she took her evening blood pressure pill twice. She's alert and talking normally right now. Here's what's on the bottle."

**To a parent who says, "Don't make a fuss":** "I hear you. I'm calling because this is the kind of thing they'd want to know about today. If it turns out to be nothing, we've lost ten minutes."

**To a parent who refuses help during a red-flag moment:** "I'm worried enough that I'm calling 911. When they get here, you can talk with them yourself." Your parent has the right to make their own decisions, but you can still call for help when you see signs of an emergency.

**To your siblings, afterward:** "Update on Dad: [what happened, when]. I called [who], and they said [what]. Next step is [X]. I've written it in the log."
