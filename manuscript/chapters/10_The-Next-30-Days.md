# The Next 30 Days

## Building a Plan That Can Actually Work

For the last few weeks, you've been reacting. A call comes in, you drop what you're doing. An appointment gets scheduled, you rearrange your week. A question comes up that nobody can answer, and you spend an evening tracking it down. It's been exhausting, but at least the urgency kept things moving.

Now your regular life is pulling you back. Work needs you. Your own family needs you. And you're wondering what happens to all of this when you can't be on call every hour of the day.

What happens is that you need a plan. Not a perfect plan or a complete one, and not a promise that everything will be sorted out in a month. Some things won't be. What you need is a short, realistic plan that says what matters most right now, who's handling it, and when you'll look at it again. That's what this chapter helps you build.

## What you know now

If you've worked through even some of this book, you know a good deal more than you did at the start. It's worth gathering it in one place before you plan.

You may have:

- A dated record of what you've noticed (Chapter 1)
- A way to sort urgent from non-urgent, and the numbers to call (Chapter 2)
- A picture of seven areas of your parent's life, with the biggest gaps marked (Chapter 3)
- A snapshot that separates what's been observed from what's been assumed, and lists what's still unknown (Chapter 4)
- Notes from recent appointments and a list of follow-ups (Chapter 5)
- A sense of which parts of daily life at home have gotten harder, and what help your parent would welcome (Chapter 6)
- An organizer with contacts, document locations, and open tasks (Chapter 7)
- A rough division of who in the family is doing what (Chapter 8)
- A list of outside help you've looked into, or want to (Chapter 9)

Don't worry if some of these are half-finished or missing. A plan built from what you have is far more useful than one that waits until you have everything.

Before you go further, ask yourself four questions and write a sentence or two for each:

1. What's actually going on? In plain words, what's your parent's situation right now?
2. What does my parent want? What have they said matters most to them?
3. What's working? What support is already in place and doing its job?
4. What isn't? Where are the gaps, the strain, or the things nobody's handling?

Those four answers are the foundation for everything that follows.

## Choose a few priorities

The most common mistake at this stage is trying to fix everything. A plan with twenty priorities is really a plan with none. Pick no more than three things that matter most over the next month.

A few questions help you choose:

- Could this cause serious harm soon if it's left alone?
- Would it be hard to undo if it goes wrong?
- Is there a real deadline, such as a follow-up appointment that needs booking, a restriction that's ending, or a waiting list to get onto?
- Is it what your parent cares about most? Their priorities belong on this list, not only yours.
- Will solving it make other things easier? Sometimes one change, like reliable transportation or a clear medication list, unlocks several others.

Everything else still matters. It just goes on the list for later, which is where it belongs for now. Writing it down and deliberately setting it aside is different from forgetting it.

## Sort everything else by time, loosely

Once you have your priorities, the rest of what you've collected can be sorted into rough time frames:

- Now. Anything urgent. If something on your list is a red-flag concern from Chapter 2, it doesn't belong on a 30-day schedule at all. It gets handled today. Yellow items get a call today or tomorrow.
- This week. Calls to make, appointments to book, one conversation to start.
- The next two weeks. Follow-ups, a trial of one support service, gathering a few more pieces of information.
- By the end of the month. Things that take a little longer: a family call, a fuller update of the organizer, checking how a new arrangement is working.
- Later. Important things that don't need to happen this month. Keep them on the list so they don't get lost.

These time frames are a guide, not a set of deadlines. Real life won't follow them neatly. A service you call may have a three-week waiting list. Your parent may want more time to think about something. A new problem may jump to the front of the line. When that happens, move things around. The point of the time frames is to keep everything from feeling equally urgent, not to create new pressure.

## Put a name on everything

Every item on the plan needs an owner, someone who will make sure it happens. That doesn't mean you.

Some items belong to your parent, who may well still be handling most of their own life. Some belong to siblings or other family members, as you worked out in Chapter 8. Some belong to outside help: the volunteer driver, the home health agency, the pharmacist who's reviewing the medications. And some belong to you.

A useful distinction: there's a difference between who *could* do something and who *will*. Plenty of people could make a phone call to the Area Agency on Aging. The plan should say who will, and by when.

If you find your name next to most of the items, that's worth noticing. It may be the right arrangement for now. Or it may be a sign that something needs to move to someone else, or to outside help, or to "later."

## Keep it current without making it a second job

A plan is only useful if it stays accurate. That doesn't take much, as long as it happens regularly.

**A short weekly review.** Fifteen minutes, same time each week. Look at the plan. Cross off what's done. Move anything that's stuck. Add anything new. Update the organizer from Chapter 7 if anything has changed.

**A weekly update to the family.** One short message, as described in Chapter 8, so nobody has to ask.

**A monthly look with your parent.** What's working? What isn't? Has anything changed about what they want? This is their plan as much as yours.

**Immediate updates when something big happens.** A hospital stay, a new diagnosis, a fall, a change in medications, a change in who's available to help. Don't wait for the weekly review.

## Plans are supposed to change

A plan isn't a promise. It's your best thinking based on what you know right now, and what you know will keep changing.

You'll get new information: a doctor's appointment clarifies something, a service turns out not to be available, a sibling's schedule opens up or closes down. Your parent's needs may change, gradually or suddenly. Your parent may change their mind about something they agreed to, which is their right. An arrangement that looked good on paper may turn out not to work in practice.

None of that means the plan failed. It means the plan is doing its job, which is to give you a starting point you can adjust. Every review date is a chance to ask whether the priorities are still the right ones.

Sometimes changes add up to something bigger. If you notice several yellow-level concerns in a short period, repeated trips to the emergency room, your parent's needs growing faster than the help in place, or the main helper running out of steam, it may be time for a more fundamental rethink. That's a good moment to talk with your parent's doctor, bring in a geriatric care manager, or look more closely at the outside help described in Chapter 9.

## What may be ahead

This book has been about the beginning: making sense of the first weeks and getting organized. Depending on your family, some of what comes later may include:

- Changes in memory or behavior that make communication harder
- Questions about whether living alone is still working
- A hospital stay and the transition home afterward
- Keeping track of a growing amount of medical information
- Sharing the load more fairly among siblings
- Helping from a long distance
- Bringing in and coordinating paid help
- Protecting your own health and limits as a caregiver
- Conversations about the future, including end-of-life wishes

You don't need to prepare for all of this now. Many families face only some of it, and on very different timelines. Other books in this series take up these subjects one at a time, for when and if they become your situation. For now, it's enough to know that the habits you've started here (noticing carefully, writing things down, talking with your parent, sharing the work, and asking for help) are the same ones you'll rely on later.

## How a plan can look

To show how simple a plan can be, here's part of one for an imaginary family.

Theo's father, Ray, came home from a short hospital stay three weeks ago. Theo has been running on adrenaline ever since. When he sits down to plan, he picks three priorities: getting Ray's new medication list checked by the pharmacist, finding reliable rides to Ray's twice-weekly cardiac rehab so Theo can stop leaving work, and getting Ray's permission on file at the cardiologist's office so Theo can call with questions. Ray's own priority is getting back to his Wednesday coffee group, so that goes on the list too.

Theo's sister will call the Area Agency on Aging about transportation this week. Ray will ask for the consent form at his next appointment. Theo will do the pharmacist visit with Ray on Saturday. The coffee group turns out to be easy: one of Ray's friends offers to pick him up. Theo sets a review for the following Sunday.

A month later, the rides are covered three days out of four, the medication list is clean, and the consent form is on file. The house still needs work, and Ray still won't talk about driving. Those go on next month's list.

## Before you close this book

You don't need all the answers to make real progress. Most families who handle this well didn't start out knowing what to do. They started out paying attention, and then took the next reasonable step, and then the one after that.

You've already done much of the hardest part, which was deciding to look closely and take it seriously. You have a clearer picture than you did, a way of telling what's urgent from what can wait, a place to keep what you've learned, and some idea of who else can help. Your parent is still at the center of their own life, and you've found ways to help without taking it over.

There will be more to figure out. There usually is. But you won't be starting from nothing again.

So fill in the top of the plan. Pick your three priorities. Put a name next to each one, and a date on the calendar to look at it again. That's enough for today.
