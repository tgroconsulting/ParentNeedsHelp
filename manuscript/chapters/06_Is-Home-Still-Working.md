# Chapter 6

# Is Home Still Working?

## Looking at Everyday Independence and Support

Every time you visit, there's something else. Last month it was the gutters. This month the hallway light has been out long enough that your mother has stopped noticing, the laundry basket at the top of the stairs is full, and there's a carton of eggs in the fridge from sometime in the spring. None of it is a disaster. But it's starting to feel like a list that only gets longer.

It's natural, at that point, for your mind to jump ahead to the big question: can she really keep living here? Sometimes that question does need asking eventually. But it's rarely the right first question, and jumping to it too soon can make every conversation about the gutters feel like a conversation about moving out.

This chapter takes a smaller and usually more useful approach. Instead of asking whether your parent can stay home, it asks which parts of everyday life at home have gotten harder, what your parent thinks about that, and what kind of support might make those parts easier.

## Why home matters so much

For many older adults, staying in their own home isn't a minor preference. In AARP's 2024 Home and Community Preferences Survey, 75 percent of adults 50 and older said they want to remain in their current homes as they age, and 73 percent said they hope to stay in their communities.¹

The reasons are easy to understand once you think about them. Home is where your parent knows where everything is, down to the drawer with the rubber bands. It's their routine, their neighbors, their church or coffee shop down the street, their garden, maybe their pet. It holds decades of memories. And it's where they're in charge. In their own home, nobody tells them when to eat or what to watch.

That's worth keeping in mind every time you notice something that's slipped. To you, the full laundry basket may look like a sign of decline. To your parent, a suggestion that they can't manage their own home may feel like the first step toward losing it. When you approach these conversations as "what would help home keep working for you," you're on the same side. And for many families, that's an honest description of the goal.

## What "working" means

A home doesn't have to be spotless or run the way you'd run it to be working. The question is whether the basics of daily life are getting done in a way your parent is reasonably comfortable with: meals, getting around, getting out, keeping the house in decent shape, and staying connected to other people.

Your parent's standards matter here. Someone who never cared much about dusting isn't in decline because the shelves are dusty. Someone who always kept an immaculate kitchen and now doesn't may be telling you something. Compare to their normal, not yours. If you recorded your parent's baseline in Chapter 3, this is where it earns its keep.

## Six areas to look at

Chapter 4 helped you build an accurate picture of how ordinary days are going. Here, the focus is on where your parent may be working harder than they used to and what support might help. These six areas are common places for that to show up.

**Mobility.** Getting up from a low chair, climbing stairs, stepping in and out of the tub, carrying things from room to room or up from the basement. You might notice your parent holding onto furniture, avoiding a part of the house, or putting off tasks that involve stairs or bending.

**Meals.** Shopping, carrying groceries in, cooking, cleaning up. You might notice a nearly empty fridge, a lot of expired food, more takeout containers than usual, the same simple meal every day, or weight loss. Sometimes the difficulty is cooking. Sometimes it's getting to the store, or carrying heavy bags, or simply that cooking for one feels pointless.

**Transportation.** Getting to the store, appointments, church, or friends' homes. If your parent drives, it may be going fine, or they may be avoiding highways, night driving, or unfamiliar places. If they don't drive, the question is whether they can reliably get where they need and want to go.

**Household upkeep.** Cleaning, laundry, trash, yard work, snow, small repairs, changing light bulbs and smoke-detector batteries. These are often the first things to slide, partly because they're physically demanding and partly because they're easy to postpone.

**Accessibility.** How the house itself fits your parent's body right now. Is the bedroom upstairs while the stairs are getting harder? Are everyday items on high shelves? Is there enough light? Are there steps at the entrance? A house that worked perfectly at 60 may fit differently at 85, even if nothing about it has changed.

**Social isolation.** Who does your parent see and talk to in a typical week? The National Institute on Aging distinguishes social isolation, which is physical separation from other people, from loneliness, the distressed feeling of being alone, and notes that you can be alone without feeling lonely or feel lonely among other people. Research has linked both to health problems including high blood pressure, heart disease, depression, and cognitive decline.² Some people genuinely prefer a quiet life. The question is whether your parent's current level of contact is what they want, or what's left after other things got harder.

## Patterns, not incidents

One dirty kitchen is not a trend. Neither is one missed trash day, one meal of crackers and cheese, or one week of not leaving the house during a cold snap. Everybody has bad weeks, and older adults are no exception.

What's worth paying attention to is a pattern: the same difficulty showing up again and again, getting gradually worse, or spreading from one area into others. A few questions help separate the two:

- How often? Once, occasionally, or most of the time?
- For how long? Since last week, or for months?
- Is there an obvious reason? A cold, a sprained wrist, grief after a friend's death, a heat wave. Temporary causes often lead to temporary changes, though it's worth noticing whether things bounce back.
- Has your parent found a workaround? Someone who can't reach the top shelf anymore but has moved everything down to the counter has solved the problem. Someone who can't reach it and has stopped using what's up there hasn't.
- What happens because of it? Dusty shelves don't hurt anybody. Not eating enough, missing medications, or not being able to get out of the house for weeks has real consequences.

If you're not sure whether something is a pattern, write it down and look again next time. Patterns tend to become obvious when you see the notes side by side.

## Asking what's hard and what would help

The most useful information about what your parent needs usually comes from your parent. And the most useful kind of help is help they actually want.

Many people find it hard to admit that something has become difficult, especially to their children. Asking in a way that doesn't put them on the defensive makes a real difference.

**Ask what's gotten harder, not what they can't do.** "Is there anything around the house that's gotten to be more of a hassle lately?" invites an honest answer. "Can you still manage the stairs?" invites a defensive one.

**Ask what they'd hand off if they could.** "If you could get rid of one chore for good, which one would it be?" Many people have a ready answer, and it's often not the one you'd guess.

**Ask what kind of help they'd welcome, and what they wouldn't.** Your parent might be happy to have someone mow the lawn but not want a stranger in the house cleaning. They might accept a ride to the grocery store but not want groceries delivered, because the trip is their outing for the week. Those preferences are worth knowing and worth respecting.

**Offer choices rather than plans.** "Would it help more to have someone do the yard, or the gutters and the heavy stuff?" lets your parent decide.

**Suggest a trial.** "Want to try it for a month and see?" is much easier to say yes to than a permanent change.

**Accept "no" for now.** If your parent turns down help, that's their right. You can mention it again later, or try a different kind of help. Pushing hard usually just closes the door.

A few things you might say:

- "I'd like to help in a way that's actually useful to you, not just what I think you need. What would make the biggest difference?"
- "I notice the laundry's piling up at the top of the stairs. Is carrying it down getting to be a pain?"
- "You've always done the yard yourself. Would you want help with it this fall, or would you rather keep at it?"
- "The point isn't to take things away from you. It's to make it easier to keep doing the things you want to do here."

## What everyday support can look like

Support doesn't have to mean a big change. Much of what helps older adults stay comfortably at home is ordinary, practical, and small. The National Institute on Aging lists help with personal care, household chores, meals, transportation, and money management among the kinds of support people use to keep living at home, and notes that family, friends, and neighbors are the biggest source of help for many older people.³

Some examples:

| Area | Support some families use |
| --- | --- |
| Shopping and meals | A standing weekly grocery trip together, grocery pickup or delivery, cooking extra and freezing portions, home-delivered meals |
| Transportation | A family ride schedule, rides from friends or a faith community, volunteer driver programs, community or senior transportation services |
| Household upkeep | Hiring help with cleaning, yard work, snow, or gutters; a handyman for small repairs; a family member taking over one specific job |
| Mobility and accessibility | Moving everyday items to easy-to-reach places, brighter bulbs and night lights, keeping paths clear; asking a professional about equipment before buying any |
| Social connection | Regular phone or video calls on a set schedule, rides to clubs or services, senior center activities, adult day programs, volunteer visitor programs |
| Paperwork and bills | Setting up automatic payments together, a monthly sit-down to go through the mail |

Many communities also have formal services. Area Agencies on Aging, which are designated by each state to serve older adults locally, coordinate and offer services that help older adults stay in their homes, including home-delivered meals and homemaker help.⁴ What's available, who qualifies, and what it costs vary from place to place. Your local Area Agency on Aging can tell you what exists near your parent. You can find it through the Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116 or eldercare.acl.gov (see Chapter 4).

## When a professional should take a look

You can notice a lot, but some questions are beyond what a family member can judge. Whether a home is safe for a particular person, whether someone can manage on their own, and what changes to a home would actually help are questions for professionals who can evaluate both the person and the space.

It may be time to ask your parent's doctor about a professional assessment when:

- Your parent has fallen, or seems unsteady or worried about falling. The CDC advises telling a healthcare provider right away if a loved one has fallen, is worried about falling, or seems unsteady.⁵ The National Institute on Aging notes that after a fall, a doctor might suggest that an occupational therapist, physical therapist, or nurse visit the home to assess its safety and suggest changes.⁶
- Daily tasks have gotten noticeably harder after an illness, surgery, or hospital stay. Ask whether therapy or home health services make sense.
- You're thinking about equipment or changes to the house. Grab bars, shower chairs, stair lifts, and ramps can help, but the right choice depends on the person. Ask before buying.
- Changes in memory or thinking are affecting everyday tasks, such as leaving the stove on or forgetting to eat.
- Family members disagree about whether your parent is managing. An outside view can help everyone, including your parent, see the situation more clearly.

The usual starting point is your parent's primary care doctor, who can refer them for an evaluation. A geriatric care manager (Chapter 4) can also help when the picture is complicated.

## Start with one or two changes

Once you've looked around and talked with your parent, it's tempting to fix everything. Resist that. One or two well-chosen changes are more likely to be accepted, more likely to stick, and easier to adjust if they don't work.

To choose:

1. Start with what your parent finds hardest, or what they said they'd most like help with.
2. Weigh what has the biggest consequences. Not eating well or not getting to appointments matters more than an untidy garage.
3. Pick something easy to start and easy to undo. A trial month of grocery pickup is a much smaller step than a cleaning service three days a week.
4. Agree on when you'll check in. "Let's see how it goes for a few weeks and then talk about it."

Here's a made-up example. A father mentions that carrying laundry up and down the basement stairs has gotten hard, and his daughter has noticed he's been wearing the same few shirts. He doesn't want anyone doing his laundry. They agree to try two small things: she'll carry the full basket down when she visits on Sundays, and they'll move a hamper to the bedroom so he isn't carrying clothes around the house. After a month, he says it's working, and he's started asking her about the gutters. One small change made the next conversation easier.

Some situations do call for bigger decisions eventually: more help at home, changes to the house, or a different living arrangement. Those are real conversations, and a later book in this series, *Is It Still Safe for Mom or Dad to Live Alone?*, takes them on in depth. For now, the goal is a clearer sense of where support would help, and a first step your parent agrees to.
