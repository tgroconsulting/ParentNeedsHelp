# Chapter 9

# Knowing When to Ask for Help

## Understanding What Family Can and Cannot Do Alone

It's the third time this month you've left work early. Once for a doctor's appointment, once because your father couldn't reach anyone about a prescription, once because a neighbor called to say his porch light had been on all day and he wasn't answering the phone. He was fine; he'd been napping. Your manager has been understanding so far. You're not sure how long "so far" lasts.

Somewhere in the back of your mind is the thought that if you were better organized, or more patient, or lived closer, you could handle all of this yourself. And alongside it is a second thought, which is that you don't actually know what other help exists, or where you'd even start looking.

This chapter is about that second thought. Outside help isn't a sign that the family has failed. In most situations, it's part of a plan that works. The goal here is to help you figure out which parts of the load could reasonably go to someone else, where to start looking, and what to ask when you do.

## Why it grows

Caregiving rarely arrives as one big job. It comes in pieces. A ride to an appointment. A call to the pharmacy. Checking in on Sunday nights. Then the gutters, the insurance letter, the follow-up visit, the new medication, a second specialist. Each piece is reasonable on its own. Together, over months, they can add up to something no one would have agreed to if it had been described all at once.

It also tends to grow unevenly. Needs change after an illness or a fall. A sibling's job changes. The neighbor who used to check in moves away. And because most of this doesn't come with an end date, a temporary arrangement can quietly become permanent.

That's not a failure of planning. It's just how this usually goes. The useful response is to look at the whole load from time to time and ask which parts still make sense for the family to carry.

## What family is best at, and what it doesn't have to do

Some things only family can do, or are things your parent specifically wants from family: knowing them well, noticing when something's off, advocating for them, being the familiar face at an important appointment, simply being there. Those are usually worth protecting.

Other things family can do, but only at a real cost: leaving work again, driving four hours each way, doing heavy chores while your own back is complaining. And some things require skills, training, or licensing that family members don't have, such as skilled nursing or therapy.

When you look at your list of tasks (the Family Coordination Worksheet from Chapter 8 is a good starting point), a few questions help sort them:

- Could we keep doing this for the next six months? If the honest answer is no, look for another way.
- Does it require skills or training we don't have? If so, it belongs with someone who has them.
- What does my parent prefer? Some parents would much rather have a hired helper with bathing than have their son do it. Others feel the opposite.
- What happens when the person doing it is sick, traveling, or burned out? If the answer is "nobody does it," that task needs a backup, and the backup may be outside the family.
- Is anyone doing it at all? Gaps, like the follow-up that never gets scheduled or the house that hasn't been cleaned in months, are often the first place outside help can make a difference.

Your parent's input matters throughout. Outside help in their home, their schedule, or their care is something they should agree to, and many are more open to it than their children expect, especially when it's framed as making it easier to keep living the way they want.

## If work is part of the problem

For many adult children, the pressure point is a job. Some legal protections may apply. According to the U.S. Department of Labor, the Family and Medical Leave Act entitles eligible employees to up to twelve workweeks of unpaid, job-protected leave in a twelve-month period to care for a parent with a serious health condition.¹ Whether you're eligible depends on your employer and your work history, and some states have their own leave laws. Your human resources department can tell you what applies to you, and the Department of Labor's website explains the federal rules in detail.

You can also ask your employer whether they offer flexible schedules, remote work, or an employee assistance program. These vary widely, but you won't know until you ask.

## The kinds of help that exist

What's available depends heavily on where your parent lives. But most communities have some version of the following.

**Transportation.** Volunteer driver programs, senior or community transportation services, rides arranged through faith communities, and paratransit for people who qualify. The Eldercare Locator can help you find transportation and in-home help in your parent's area.²

**Meal programs.** Home-delivered meals, often called "meals on wheels" programs, and community meal programs at senior centers or other sites. Area Agencies on Aging coordinate and offer services that help older adults stay at home, including home-delivered meals.³

**Community aging services.** Your parent's local Area Agency on Aging is often the hub for information about services in the area, from meals and homemaker help to caregiver programs (Chapter 4 explains how to find it). Senior centers may offer activities, meals, exercise classes, and help connecting with other services.

**Help at home.** "Home care" can mean two quite different things.

- *Home health care* is medical care at home, such as skilled nursing or physical therapy, usually ordered by a doctor. Medicare covers home health services under certain conditions, including that the person is homebound, needs part-time or intermittent skilled care, and has been seen by a doctor or other provider who certifies the need. Medicare doesn't cover 24-hour care at home, home meal delivery, homemaker services such as shopping and cleaning that are unrelated to the care plan, or personal care when that's the only care someone needs.⁴
- *Non-medical home care* covers help with daily activities, such as bathing, dressing, light housekeeping, meals, errands, and companionship. It's often paid for privately, though some programs and insurance plans may help. Ask each provider what payment they accept.

**Adult day programs.** Daytime programs that offer activities, meals, and supervision in a group setting, which can give an older adult social contact and a family caregiver time to work or rest.

**Help for the caregiver.** Programs exist specifically to support family caregivers. The National Family Caregiver Support Program, funded through the federal Administration for Community Living, supports family members caring for adults 60 and older with information about available services, help getting access to them, individual counseling, support groups, caregiver training, respite care, and limited supplemental services.⁵ Respite care is short-term relief for a primary caregiver, lasting anywhere from a few hours to several weeks, and can happen at home, in a care facility, or at an adult day center. The National Institute on Aging notes that respite from friends, relatives, or volunteers may cost nothing, that professional services charge by the hour or day, and that most private health insurance doesn't cover respite care.⁶

**Help understanding insurance.** If your parent has Medicare and you or they have questions about coverage, your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) offers free, unbiased counseling to people with Medicare and their families.⁷ SHIP counselors can help your parent understand their options. This book doesn't advise on insurance choices, but SHIP can.

## Where to start looking

The hardest part is often knowing which sources to trust. Start with these:

- Your parent's Area Agency on Aging, through the Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116 or eldercare.acl.gov.²
- 211. Dialing 211 connects you with a local information and referral service run by community organizations. According to 211.org, trained specialists are available around the clock, and calls are confidential.⁸
- Your parent's State Health Insurance Assistance Program for Medicare questions, at shiphelp.org.⁷
- Your parent's doctor, hospital social worker, or discharge planner. They often know local services and can make referrals, especially after a hospital stay.
- Medicare's Care Compare tool at medicare.gov/care-compare, which lists Medicare-certified providers, including home health agencies, in a given area.⁹
- Your parent's faith community, senior center, or neighbors, who often know about informal help and local programs.
- Your parent's state agency on aging, which can tell you about state programs and, where they exist, licensing requirements for home care providers.

A word of caution about general internet searches. Some websites that offer to match families with care providers are businesses that may be paid by the providers they recommend. That doesn't make them bad, but it's worth asking any referral service how it's paid before relying on its suggestions.

## What to ask when you call

Whether you're calling a community program or a private provider, a few questions get you a long way. You won't need all of them every time.

**About the service:**
- What exactly do you provide, and what don't you provide?
- What area do you serve?
- Who is eligible? Are there age, income, health, or residency requirements?
- Is there a waiting list? How long is it right now?
- How do we get started? Does my parent need a referral or an assessment?

**About cost:**
- What does it cost, and how is it billed?
- Do you accept Medicare, Medicaid, or other insurance, and for which services?
- Is it free, donation-based, or on a sliding scale?
- Are there minimum hours, contracts, deposits, or cancellation fees?

**About people and quality (especially for in-home help):**
- Are you licensed or certified, and by whom? (Requirements vary by state.)
- How are staff screened and trained? Do you do background checks?
- Who supervises the people who come into the home?
- What happens if a caregiver is sick or doesn't show up?
- Who do we call with a concern or complaint?
- Can you provide references?

**About fit:**
- Can my parent meet the person, or try the service before committing?
- How do you handle a situation where my parent isn't comfortable with someone?

Write down the answers, the name of the person you spoke with, and the date. That last part matters more than it seems.

## Why everything needs checking locally, and recently

This book can't tell you which programs exist near your parent, who qualifies, what they cost, or how long the waiting list is. Those things vary from state to state and county to county, and they change: programs gain or lose funding, eligibility rules are updated, prices go up, providers open and close.

So treat everything you find, including what's in this chapter, as a starting point to confirm:

- Ask directly, and ask about now. "Is that still available?" "Is that the current price?" "What's the wait as of today?"
- Write down the date and who told you. Information from six months ago may no longer be accurate.
- Get important details in writing, especially cost, services included, and cancellation terms.
- Check credentials yourself where it matters, such as confirming a home health agency is Medicare-certified or a home care agency meets your state's licensing rules.
- Don't assume one county's program exists in the next. If your parent lives in a different state from you, what's available to you locally may not be available to them.

## Start with one

You don't need to line up five services at once. Pick one area where outside help could make the most immediate difference, and start there.

To choose, look at:

- What's costing the most in time off work, travel, or strain on one person.
- What's most at risk of not getting done.
- What your parent would accept. A service your parent refuses won't help.
- What's easiest to try. A volunteer driver for one weekly appointment is a smaller first step than daily in-home help.

Consider a hypothetical caregiver named Marisol, who has been leaving work every Tuesday and Thursday to drive her mother to physical therapy across town. Her mother doesn't want anyone in her house but doesn't mind who drives her. Marisol calls the Eldercare Locator, which connects her with her mother's Area Agency on Aging. They tell her about a volunteer driver program run by a local nonprofit. There's a two-week wait. She signs her mother up, keeps driving in the meantime, and after the first month the volunteer covers Thursdays. One call didn't solve everything, but it gave her back one afternoon a week.

Evaluating and coordinating outside care in more depth, including comparing providers and managing paid help over time, is the subject of a later book in this series, *When Family Help Isn't Enough*.

## One call

Pick the task that's costing you the most, and make one call about it this week: to the Eldercare Locator, 211, or your parent's doctor's office. Write down who you talked to and what they said. Even if the answer is "not here," you'll know where to try next.
