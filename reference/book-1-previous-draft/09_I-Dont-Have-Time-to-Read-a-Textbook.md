# Chapter 9: I Don't Have Time to Read a Textbook

*Day 30, and every week after*

**The two-minute version.** If you read nothing else:

1. Fill in **one dashboard a week**. It takes ten minutes.
2. Give every task **one owner**, with a name and a date.
3. Fill in the **prep sheet** before every appointment.
4. When something is off, use the **decision trees**. Red means call 911, yellow means call the doctor today, and green means write it down.
5. Write the **handoff sheet** while things are calm, not on the worst day.

## 1. The frustration

You're in your car at lunch. The pharmacy has called, your sister has texted, the school has emailed, and you're scanning the index of a 300-page caregiving book for "what to do when Dad stops eating." You don't have time for a chapter. You need a page.

The load is real. In *Caregiving in the US 2025*, AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving found that people caring for an adult 50 or older spend an average of 26 hours a week on it, up from 22 in 2020. Six in 10 family caregivers are employed, and half of those report work disruptions. Just over 20% have had any formal training on medical or nursing tasks, though over half manage them. You don't need more information. You need less of it, in the right place.

## 2. What may be happening

- **Books are organized by topic. Your problems arrive by urgency.** The answer exists, but you can't find it fast enough to use it.
- **The system is you.** Appointments, refill dates, and who promised what live in one head. That works until it doesn't.
- **You remake the same decisions every time.** Without rules, "Should I call the doctor?" gets decided from scratch at 9 p.m. each time.

What helps is a few pages that hold the information for you.

## 3. What to do now

**The ten-minute weekly review** (Dashboard, page 1). Pick a fixed time, like Sunday evening. Spend two minutes looking back (fill in *What changed?*), three looking ahead (appointments, refills), three assigning (every open task gets one owner), and two sharing (send a photo of the page to your backup and siblings).

**Appointment prep on one sheet** (page 2). The National Institute on Aging suggests ranking your concerns and planning on three or four, because that's what a visit can realistically cover. Bring the medicines (or the bottles), insurance cards, and other doctors' names. Tell anyone going with you what you want from the visit, and ask your parent whether they'd like time alone with the doctor. Before you leave, repeat the plan back in your own words.

**Shared calendar and task ownership** (page 3). Create one calendar named for your parent, and invite siblings and your backup person. Put every appointment on it with who is driving. Pick one place for messages and one for documents. Free tools can help, such as the care calendar at Lotsa Helping Hands. The real rule is on the page: whoever claims a task owns it until they hand it off out loud.

**Tracking changes without writing a diary** (Dashboard, *What changed?*). Check Better, Same, or Worse for each area, and add one line only when something is worse. Save full entries for yellow and red events (incident log, binder page 11). Log the change, not the day.

**Simple decision trees** (pages 4 and 5). There are four:

- Something is different today.
- I can't reach my parent.
- A medicine problem.
- "I don't need help."

Stroke signs (BE-FAST: sudden trouble with Balance, Eyes, Face, Arms, or Speech) are a 911 call, even if the symptoms pass, and you should note when they started (American Stroke Association). When you're unsure, go one color up.

**An emergency handoff another person can understand** (page 6). Hospitals use structured handoffs for the same reason. In a 2014 *New England Journal of Medicine* study of structured handoffs (called I-PASS) across nine pediatric hospital residency programs, medical errors fell 23% and preventable adverse events fell 30%. Your parent's care isn't a hospital ward, but the same idea applies: use the same headings every time, and have the receiver say it back. The handoff sheet adapts the idea to five parts:

1. How stable things are
2. A short summary
3. A to-do list
4. If this happens, do this
5. A read-back

> **One family's story**
>
> Marcus and his sister used to talk about their mother in long, anxious phone calls that ended without a plan. Now they hold a 20-minute call every Sunday evening with a one-page dashboard: appointments, medication changes, new concerns, and who's doing what. Most calls end with three decisions and one owner for each.

## 4. Exactly what to say

**To your siblings, setting up the weekly update:** "Every Sunday I'll send a photo of a one-page dashboard. Read it, ask questions, and claim a task if you want one. If it's urgent, I'll call."

**To a sibling, about ownership:** "Which of these do you want to own? I'd rather you own one thing completely than help a little with everything."

**To the doctor, at the start of the visit:** "We have three things for today. The most important is that she's been dizzy when she stands up."

**To your parent, about the handoff:** "I'm writing down what a good day looks like for you, so if I'm ever away, whoever helps can keep things normal. What matters most about your mornings?"

**To the person covering for you:** "Here's the sheet. Read it back to me in your own words, and tell me what's unclear."

## 6. When professional help is appropriate

- **Your Area Agency on Aging.** Call the Eldercare Locator (1-800-677-1116, call or text). Local agencies can arrange check-ins, meals, transportation, and help with medicines.
- **A geriatric care manager.** Hire one when the coordination outgrows the family, or when you live far away. They can also keep the plan current.
- **Your own doctor or a counselor.** Emotional stress among caregivers has risen since 2020, according to the AARP/NAC release. If you're skipping your own appointments, that's the signal.
- **Your parent's doctor,** to review the decision trees and add specifics for their conditions.
