# Your Parent Needs Help—Now What?

*A 30-Day Action Plan for Doctors, Documents, Home Safety, and Family Decisions*

## A note from the author

I wrote this book for the moment when you realize something has changed and nobody has told you what to do next. A few years ago, I lost my mother. Before that, I lived through the decisions my parents had to make about her care, and afterward I watched my dad slowly work out how to live alone. This is the book I wish our family had had.

Most advice for caregivers is either too general to act on or too long to read when you're exhausted. This book tries to be neither. Every chapter ends with something you can do today, and you don't have to read it in order. Start wherever your family is right now.

## How this book works

This book takes you through the first 30 days of becoming your parent's care coordinator, then through the 60 days after that.

Every chapter has the same six parts, so you always know where you are:

1. **The frustration:** the moment you are probably in right now.
2. **What may be happening:** the pattern behind it.
3. **What to do now:** concrete steps, with day ranges.
4. **Exactly what to say:** scripts you can read aloud.
5. **A tool:** a checklist, worksheet, or printable you can use today.
6. **When professional help is appropriate:** who to call, and when.

Start at Chapter 1 and follow the days, or jump to the chapter that matches the problem in front of you. The days are a guide, not a deadline.

Each chapter also includes a short section called **One family's story**. The families you'll meet there (Dana and her father, Frank; Marcus and his mother, Ruth; and sisters Elena and Sofia Reyes and their mother, Carmen) are composites. Each is built from situations caregivers commonly describe. None is a real person, and any resemblance is coincidental. They're here so you can see the steps in this book play out in an ordinary, imperfect family.

## Chapter 1: Why Is This Suddenly My Job?

*Days 1–3 of your 30-day plan*

### 1. The frustration

*"I know something needs to change, but I don't know what I'm responsible for, or whether I'm overreacting."*

You're sitting in your car in the work parking lot, rereading a text from your sister: "Have you seen Dad's mail? It's all over the kitchen table." You have. You saw it three weeks ago, along with the dent in his bumper, the expired milk in the fridge, and the story he told you twice in one phone call. You told yourself he was tired.

Now you're wondering whether this is a real problem or whether you're a worrier turning normal aging into a crisis. Underneath that is a harder question: if it is real, whose job is it? Nobody called a meeting or handed you a folder. You noticed, and noticing is how most people become caregivers.

You're in large company. In its 2025 report, AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving estimated that 63 million Americans, nearly one in four adults, provide care for a family member or friend. About 59 million of them care for an adult, and the total is up about 20 million since 2015. Few of them applied for the role.

This chapter is the job description nobody gave you.

### 2. What may be happening

Aging rarely announces itself. It shows up as small things that each have a reasonable excuse, until the excuses stop adding up. One missed appointment is an incident. A missed appointment plus unopened mail plus a dented bumper is a pattern. Noticing a pattern isn't overreacting.

The numbers explain why waiting is risky. According to the CDC, one in four adults 65 and older reports falling each year, and falls are the leading cause of injury and of injury-related death in this age group. In a CDC survey of 2014 data, about 37% of those who fell reported an injury that needed medical treatment or limited their activity, and about half had not talked to a health care provider about the fall. Your parent may be falling more than you know.

One distinction matters right now: **speed**. Gradual change over months is a pattern to track. Sudden change is a medical question. Delirium is a sudden shift in attention, thinking, and alertness, and in older adults it can be the first sign of a medical problem. Infections, especially urinary tract infections and pneumonia, are a common cause, and so are medications. The VA notes that delirium comes on quickly, over days, while dementia comes on slowly, over months to years. Confusion that appears suddenly isn't "just getting older." Call the doctor the same day.

Two traps catch almost every new caregiver:

**Taking over.** You see a problem and fix it, whether or not anyone asked. It feels efficient, but it erodes your parent's dignity and quietly makes you responsible for everything.

**Staying out.** "It's not my place" feels respectful, but it usually means waiting for a crisis, when options are fewer and costs are higher.

The way through is knowing who controls what:

- **Your parent** controls their own life, including where they live, whom they see, which treatment they accept, and how they spend their money. Adults are presumed capable of making these choices. Forgetfulness, or a decision you disagree with, doesn't change that.
- **Doctors** make medical judgments: diagnoses, treatment recommendations, and whether a person can understand and weigh a particular decision.
- **Professionals** (attorneys, financial advisors, care managers, home care agencies) control their own areas of expertise.
- **Family** controls what each person chooses to contribute. No sibling controls another.
- **You** control your own role: what you offer, what you decline, and how you organize the effort.

Notice what's missing from that list: deciding for your parent.

### 3. What to do now

**Step 1: Name your role.** Say it plainly: *I coordinate help. I don't run a life.* A coordinator gathers information, connects people, and follows up. Your parent decides.

**Step 2: Write down what you've seen.** Spend ten minutes listing specifics with dates. "Dad seems off" is hard for a doctor to use. "March 3: missed his cardiology appointment; March 10: three unpaid bills; March 14: told me the same story twice" gets attention.

**Step 3: Sort the problem.** For each issue, ask: *whose decision is this?* If the answer is "my parent's," your job is to inform and support, not to override.

**Step 4: Set your limits before you're exhausted.** Caregivers who skip this step tend to pay for it. In AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving's 2025 report, nearly one in four caregivers provided 40 or more hours of care per week, about one in five reported fair or poor health, and nearly half reported at least one major financial impact, such as taking on debt.

**Step 5: Know what you're not going to get from Medicare.** Many families assume it will pay for ongoing help. Medicare doesn't pay for long-term care, meaning help with bathing, dressing, and using the bathroom, whether at home, in assisted living, or in a nursing home. Short-term rehab after a hospital stay is a different category. For scale, CareScout's 2025 Cost of Care Survey (released March 2026) reported national medians of $35 an hour for an in-home non-medical caregiver, $74,400 a year for assisted living, and $114,975 a year for a semi-private nursing home room. Your area may differ. Knowing this now will change a lot of what you decide later.

**Step 6: Tell your siblings.** Send a short, factual update now, even if they're distant.

> **One family's story**
>
> Dana, 52, lives 20 minutes from her widowed father, Frank, 81. Her brother lives in Denver. Because she's closest, she becomes the default caregiver without anyone deciding it.
>
> When Dana finds a stack of overdue bills on Frank's kitchen table, she quietly starts paying them online. Frank discovers it and is furious that she went into his accounts. After a few days, she apologizes and tries again: "I'd like to help with the bills. Do you want me to, or would you rather we set up autopay together?" He chooses autopay. Dana realizes her job is to coordinate, not to take over.

### 4. Exactly what to say

**To your parent, opening the conversation:** "Dad, I'm not here to take anything over. I've noticed a few things, and I'd rather ask you than guess. Can I tell you what I've seen, and you tell me if I've got it wrong?"

**When they say "I'm fine":** "I believe you want to be fine, and I'm not saying you're not. I'd just feel better if we checked. Would you rather start with a doctor visit, or with me going through the mail with you?"

*Offering a choice between two small steps keeps the decision in their hands.*

**To your siblings:** "I want everyone in the loop. Here's what I've seen: \[two or three dated specifics\]. I'm willing to coordinate, but I can't do everything. Can we get on a call this week and divide this up?"

**To anyone who assumes you'll handle it all:** "I can take on \[specific task\]. I can't take on \[specific task\]. Let's figure out who can."

### 6. When professional help is appropriate

**Call 911** for chest pain, trouble breathing, or signs of a stroke such as a drooping face, slurred speech, or sudden weakness.

**Call the doctor the same day** for sudden confusion, fever, a new fall, or medication mix-ups.

**Call a geriatric care manager** (usually a nurse or social worker) if the situation feels tangled and you need someone to assess needs and map options.

**Call an elder law attorney** if you're unsure about powers of attorney, health care directives, or protecting assets. Chapter 3 covers documents.

**Call the Eldercare Locator** at 1-800-677-1116 to find your parent's local Area Agency on Aging. It's a free public service of the Administration for Community Living.

**Seek counseling or a caregiver support group** if guilt, resentment, or exhaustion is building. Looking after yourself is part of the job.

You've named your role. Next comes your first hard judgment call: whether what you're seeing is an emergency or just a bad week.

## Chapter 2: Is This an Emergency or Just a Bad Week?

*Days 3–5 of your 30-day plan*

### 1. The frustration

*"Every problem feels urgent, and I can't tell what needs attention today versus next month."*

It's Sunday, 8:40 p.m. Your mom's cough sounds worse than it did on Friday. Your dad's blood pressure refill is two days late. Your brother has texted, "Should we take her in?" A neighbor mentioned your dad seemed unsteady at the mailbox. All of it arrives at the same volume.

So you do one of two things. You rush to the ER over a cold, or you tell yourself it's probably nothing and go to bed, and then you lie awake. Both are what happens when you have worries but no system for sorting them.

The three-color system below is meant to fill that gap.

### 2. What may be happening

Part of why everything feels urgent is that you don't have a baseline yet. You don't know what "normal" looks like for your parent's walking, thinking, eating, and sleeping, so every change looks equally alarming.

The other part is that older adults don't follow the textbook, which means a bad week can be an emergency in disguise. Heart trouble in older adults is a good example. A 2022 American Heart Association scientific statement notes that heart attacks in people 75 and older are more likely to happen without chest pain, and may show up as shortness of breath, fainting, or sudden confusion. Infection works the same way. Older adults may not run a high fever with a serious infection. A urinary tract infection can cause sudden confusion without the usual urinary symptoms. That matters because infection can progress to sepsis, and in 2008 U.S. hospital data, adults 65 and older were 13 times more likely than younger adults to be hospitalized with it.

Medications are another hidden risk. A CDC-led study of 2007–2009 data from 58 hospitals, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2011, estimated nearly 100,000 emergency hospitalizations a year for adverse drug events in adults 65 and older. Four kinds of medicine (warfarin, insulin, antiplatelet drugs, and diabetes pills) accounted for 67% of them, and about 66% involved unintentional overdoses.

The reverse is also true. Most bad weeks are just bad weeks. Your job is to sort quickly and consistently, so you act fast on the real emergencies without letting every worry run your life. You don't need to be a nurse to do that.

*A note: this chapter is a guide, not a diagnosis. When you're unsure, call 911. The dispatcher will help you decide.*

### 3. What to do now

Use three colors. For anything that worries you, ask: *Is this sudden? Could it cause serious harm in the next few hours? Is it different from my parent's normal?*

#### 🔴 RED: Call 911 now

- **Stroke signs (BE FAST):** sudden trouble with balance or vision, facial drooping, arm weakness, or slurred speech. Call 911 even if the symptoms go away. Note the time they started. Don't drive your parent yourself, because paramedics can alert the hospital so the stroke team is ready.
- **Chest pressure, unexplained trouble breathing, or sudden confusion with dizziness**
- **Suspected sepsis:** an infection plus confusion, fast breathing, extreme fatigue, clammy skin, or severe pain.
- **A fall with a head strike while on blood thinners.** Seek emergency care even if symptoms seem mild, and call 911 for any weakness, numbness, slurred speech, or new confusion.
- **A fall where they can't get up,** or have severe pain or an obvious deformity
- **Talk of suicide with a plan or means.** Call 911 if someone may be in imminent danger.

#### 🟡 YELLOW: Call a clinician today

- **A sudden change in thinking or alertness** without red-flag signs. This is the delirium pattern from Chapter 1.
- **Any fall,** even without injury. A head bump without blood thinners still deserves a same-day call, and the doctor may want to see your parent in person.
- **A medication mix-up:** double doses, skipped insulin or blood thinners, wrong pills. Call the pharmacist or Poison Help. The free, confidential line, 1-800-222-1222, is open around the clock and staffed by nurses, pharmacists, and doctors. HRSA says people usually get the help they need over the phone.
- **Not eating or drinking for a day or more,** new urinary symptoms, or a wound that's red, warm, or worsening
- **Possible exploitation or mistreatment.** You don't need proof to report to Adult Protective Services. For a suspected scam, the National Elder Fraud Hotline is 1-833-372-8311.
- **Hopelessness, giving things away, or saying goodbye.** Dialing or texting 988 connects family and caregivers, not just the person in crisis, to counselors around the clock. In 2024, men 75 and older had the highest suicide rate of any age group, 40.1 deaths per 100,000, according to the National Center for Health Statistics.

#### 🟢 GREEN: Goes into the 30-day plan

Piled-up mail, forgetfulness that has crept in over months, a cluttered house, hearing or vision decline, a single missed appointment, loneliness, sibling friction, no legal documents. Green doesn't mean ignore. It means *log it and schedule it.*

**The tiebreaker: when in doubt, go one color up.** Being wrong in that direction costs you an embarrassing phone call. Being wrong the other way can cost far more.

> **One family's story**
>
> Marcus, 46, lives 700 miles from his mother, Ruth, 78, who lives alone. He sees her twice a year and relies on phone calls and a helpful neighbor.
>
> On a Sunday, the neighbor calls: Ruth seems confused and keeps asking what day it is. Marcus assumes her memory is finally slipping and plans to raise it at her next checkup. Then he remembers that this came on suddenly, which makes it yellow, not green. He calls her doctor's after-hours line. It's a urinary tract infection, and within a week of antibiotics Ruth is herself again.

### 4. Exactly what to say

**To a 911 dispatcher:** "My father is 82. Since about 2:15 he's had slurred speech and his left arm is weak. He takes a blood thinner. We're at \[address\]."

**To the ER triage nurse:** "Mom was walking and talking normally yesterday. Today she's confused and sleepy. She had a urinary infection in the spring. I'm concerned about sepsis." Naming sepsis out loud may prompt staff to check for it.

**To the doctor's office (yellow):** "I'm calling about my mother, \[name, birthdate\]. Since yesterday she's been more confused than usual, which is a sudden change for her. She takes \[medications\]. I'd like her seen today. If that's not possible, what should we do?"

**To a parent who says "Don't make a fuss":** "I'm not overruling you. I'm calling because this is the kind of thing doctors want to hear about right away. If I'm wrong, we've lost ten minutes."

**To a parent refusing 911 during a red-flag moment:** "I know you don't want to go. I'm having the paramedics check you out. You can decide what happens after they're here."

**To your siblings, afterward:** "Update: \[what happened, when\]. \[Who we called, what they said\]. Next step is \[X\]. Logged in the shared file."

### 6. When professional help is appropriate

- **Any red flag:** 911. Don't wait to see if it passes.
- **Yellow:** the doctor's office the same day. If there's no appointment, ask for the nurse line or an urgent care referral.
- **Medication confusion:** a pharmacist can review every prescription and supplement, and Poison Help answers urgent questions.
- **Several yellows in a month:** that's a pattern. Ask the doctor about a geriatric assessment, or consider a geriatric care manager.
- **Suspected abuse, neglect, or severe self-neglect:** contact Adult Protective Services in the state where your parent lives. The Eldercare Locator can help you find the right office.
- **You:** if you're not sleeping, a counselor or caregiver support group is part of the plan.

If you only do one thing this week, save the phone numbers and write the Grab Sheet.

## Chapter 3: Everyone Says "Get Organized," But Organize What?

*Days 6–10 of your 30-day plan*

### 1. The frustration

*"The advice sounds like common sense, but nobody tells me exactly what information I need."*

A friend says, "You just need to get organized." A website says, "Gather important documents." So you buy a three-ring binder, write MOM on the spine, and sit down with it. Then you stall. Which documents? From where? Does "important" include the electric bill? The binder stays empty, and now you feel behind on something you can't even define.

The advice is fine as far as it goes. It just stops too soon. What follows covers seven specific areas, what to find in each, and how to find it without turning every visit into a deposition.

### 2. What may be happening

Generic advice fails in three ways. It doesn't say what to collect, it treats every gap as equally urgent, and it assumes you can simply ask. Your parent may not know where things are, may be embarrassed that they don't, or may hear "Where's your will?" as "I'm planning your funeral."

You're building two things:

**A picture.** That means facts (names, numbers, documents) plus observations of how your parent actually functions. Professionals need both. "Dad's medications" is vague. "Dad takes eleven pills a day from three pharmacies" is something a doctor can act on.

**A ranking.** You won't fix seven areas in a month, so you need to know which three gaps could do the most harm soonest.

One area runs on a clock the others don't. Legal documents must be signed while your parent can still understand them. A dementia diagnosis doesn't automatically bar someone from signing, because what matters is capacity at the time, but if someone becomes incapacitated without a power of attorney, a court may have to appoint a guardian. That's why legal paperwork gets early attention even when everything else looks calm.

### 3. What to do now

#### The seven areas

**1. Health.** Collect diagnoses, doctors' names and numbers, and insurance cards (Medicare, any supplement or Advantage plan, drug plan). Get written permission to talk to the doctors. Under the HIPAA privacy rule, providers aren't required to share information with family, though they may if you're involved in care and your parent doesn't object (HHS). Even if a provider can't tell you anything, nothing stops you from telling them what you've seen.

**2. Medications.** List every prescription, over-the-counter product, and supplement. In a 2020 University of Michigan National Poll on Healthy Aging of adults 50 to 80, about two in ten took five or more prescription medicines, and only 29% of those people had ever had a comprehensive medication review.

**3. Daily functioning.** Track two lists. The basic activities are feeding, continence, transferring, toileting, dressing, and bathing. The second list covers meals, shopping, medications, money, phone use, and transportation. Pay attention to the second list, because these more complex tasks typically decline before basic self-care does. Missed bills and pills often show up before bathing trouble.

**4. Home safety.** Walk through as a guest. CDC says falls are often caused by hazards that are easy to overlook but easy to fix. Look for loose rugs, clutter, stairs without handrails on both sides, a dark path from bed to bathroom, and no phone within reach at floor level.

**5. Transportation.** Is your parent still driving? Look for new dents, tickets, or getting lost. The CDC reports that about 9,100 older adults were killed in crashes in 2022, roughly 25 a day. The Insurance Institute for Highway Safety says the higher death rates for drivers 70 and older are mainly due to greater vulnerability to injury, not to being in more crashes. So this is about protecting your parent, not blaming them. Also ask who gets them to appointments if they stop driving.

**6. Finances.** Find income sources, regular bills, accounts, and where statements are kept. Watch for unopened mail or unusual withdrawals. In 2025, people 60 and older reported about $7.7 billion in fraud losses to the FBI, roughly 60% more than in 2024 (FBI figures as reported by AARP in April 2026). A "trusted contact" at the bank is one safeguard. A trusted contact generally can't make trades or decisions on the account. They're the person the institution can call if it sees signs of exploitation, and details vary by institution.

**7. Legal documents.** You're looking for a health care power of attorney, a financial power of attorney, a living will, a will, and the location of the originals. These are separate documents, because a financial power of attorney differs from a health care power of attorney, which covers only medical decisions. Gaps are common. A 2017 Health Affairs review of 150 studies conducted from 2011 to 2016 found that 36.7% of U.S. adults had completed an advance directive, and even among those 65 and older, the figure was 45.6%.

#### Finding missing information without interrogating

- **Ask "where," not "what."** "Where do you keep your insurance cards?" is easier than "What insurance do you have?"
- **Borrow a reason.** "I'm updating my own paperwork and realized I don't have yours."
- **Look, don't inspect.** Notice the mail pile, the pill bottles, the fridge, and the car, then write it down in the car afterward.
- **Go along.** Ask to join one appointment, and ask the pharmacy for a printed medication list.
- **One question per visit.** Small asks feel like help. A list of twenty feels like an audit.
- **Use other witnesses.** Siblings, neighbors, and longtime friends often know things your parent hasn't told you.

#### Naming your three biggest risks

Rate each area 🟢 fine for now, 🟡 needs a plan this month, or 🔴 likely harm within weeks. Then pick no more than three, using these questions. Could this cause serious harm soon? Is it hard to undo? Is there a deadline, like the capacity window? Those three drive your 30-day plan. Everything else waits.

#### Turning observations into notes for professionals

Write what happened, when, and how often, without a diagnosis. Compare "Mom is forgetful" with "Since March, Mom has missed three appointments, paid the water bill twice, and called twice in one evening to ask the same question." The second version gives a doctor something to work with. Keep it to one page and attach the medication list.

> **One family's story**
>
> Carmen Reyes, 84, lives near her daughter Elena, who does most of the hands-on help. Her other daughter, Sofia, lives across the country.
>
> Helping Carmen look for her insurance card, Elena comes across her mother's power of attorney. It was signed in 1998 and names Elena's father, who died six years ago. Nobody else is named as a backup. The sisters book an elder law attorney that month, while Carmen can still understand and sign new documents.

### 4. Exactly what to say

**About papers:** "Mom, I'm getting my own paperwork in order, and it made me realize I don't know where yours is. You don't have to show me anything. Could you just tell me where you keep it, so I'd know where to look if something happened?"

**About the bank:** "I'm not asking about your money. There's an option called a trusted contact. I'd have no access to your accounts. The bank would only call me if something looked like a scam. Would you be okay with that?"

**About medications:** "Would you do something with me? We put every bottle and supplement in a bag and let the pharmacist look it over. Your plan may even cover it."

**About doctors:** "Would you sign a form so Dr. Lee can talk to me and I can see your portal? You'd still make every decision. I'd just stop having to guess."

**When they say "That's private":** "That's fair, and I won't push. Can we agree on one person who knows where everything is if you're ever in the hospital? It can be me, your sister, or your attorney."

**To a professional:** "I've put together a one-page summary of what I've noticed. I'm not asking you to diagnose anything. I'd like to know what you think we should check first."

## Chapter 4: I Have a List—What Do I Actually Do With It?

*Days 11–14 of your 30-day plan*

### 1. The frustration

*"I understand the problems. I still don't know what action to take Monday morning."*

It's Monday, 7:15 a.m. Your coffee is going cold next to the assessment from Chapter 3. You've rated seven areas. Your three biggest risks are circled. You have twenty minutes before your first meeting, and you stare at a list that says things like *fix medications, make the house safer, get legal documents.*

Each item is a project, not a task. So you do what most people do, which is nothing, or something random like a Saturday spent rearranging your mother's pantry while the unpaid bills sit in the drawer.

What you need is a list of actions small enough to start today.

### 2. What may be happening

Lists freeze people when the items are the wrong size. "Fix Dad's medications" has no first step. "Photograph every bottle label on Sunday" does. A concern is a worry. An action is something you could do with your hands or your phone in under thirty minutes.

Specific plans work better than good intentions. A 2006 analysis by Gollwitzer and Sheeran, covering 94 independent tests, found that plans spelling out the when, where, and how of acting in advance had a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment. That's why every action in this chapter gets an owner, a date, and a trigger.

Two other forces work against you. The first is that medical appointments, where many of your actions run, leak information. A 2003 review in the Journal of the Royal Society of Medicine found that patients forget 40 to 80% of the medical information they are given almost immediately, and that almost half of what they do remember is incorrect. The second is that referrals leak too. In a study of one large health system covering July 2015 to June 2016, only about 35% of referral scheduling attempts ended in a documented completed appointment. That was a single system, but the point stands: if nobody tracks the referral, it can quietly disappear. Often that somebody is you.

Finally, don't try to repair everything at once. Your parent can absorb only so much change, and you can only do so much before you burn out. Doing things in order is how they actually get finished.

### 3. What to do now

**Step 1: Convert each concern into a next action.** A good next action is physical, visible, and under thirty minutes.

| Concern | Next action |
| --- | --- |
| "Dad's medications are a mess." | Photograph every bottle label Sunday. Ask the pharmacy for a printed list. |
| "Mom may not be safe in the shower." | Ask her doctor's office for an occupational therapy home-safety referral. |
| "Bills are piling up." | Sit with Mom for 30 minutes and open one week's mail together. |
| "There are no legal documents." | Call two elder law offices and ask about fees and the first opening. |
| "Dad's driving worries me." | Ride along Thursday and write down three specific observations. |

**Step 2: Give every action an owner, a date, and a trigger.** The owner is one name, not "we." Add a trigger: *"When I park at Mom's on Sunday, I'll photograph the bottles."* Remember Chapter 1, too. If the action involves your parent's life, your parent's role is to agree or help, not just to be informed.

**Step 3: Choose the first three.** Start from your top three risks, then filter. Can it be done in two weeks? Will your parent agree or at least not object? Can it be undone? Pick one action that's easy, because an early win builds your parent's trust. Everything else goes on a **parking lot** list with a review date. Keep no more than three actions active at once.

**Step 4: Prepare for appointments.** National Institute on Aging guidance on talking with your doctor is consistent. Make a prioritized list and bring up the biggest concerns first. Don't save what's on your mind for the end of the visit. Bring a list of every medication and supplement, or put the bottles in a bag. Bring a family member or friend who can take notes, and tell them in advance what you want from the visit. Also bring eyeglasses and hearing aids. Send your top three concerns through the patient portal beforehand if you can, and ask whether you should book a longer visit.

In the room, use teach-back. Repeat back what you think you heard. "Do you understand?" doesn't count, because real teach-back asks people to explain in their own words, not answer yes or no. If time runs out, ask to follow up by phone or email, book another visit, or speak with a nurse.

**Step 5: Track every referral until the loop closes.** A referral isn't done when it's ordered. It's done when the specialist has seen your parent and the report has reached the primary doctor. First, find out whether the plan requires one at all. Original Medicare doesn't require a referral to see a specialist, but Medicare Advantage plans may require a referral and prior authorization. Rules shift. For example, UnitedHealthcare announced in September 2025 that, starting January 1, 2026, most of its Medicare Advantage HMO and HMO-POS members would need a primary care referral for certain specialist services. Then, within three business days, call the specialist to confirm receipt, book the date, and log it.

**Step 6: Hold a 20-minute weekly review.** Every Sunday, check what's done, what's stuck, and whether anything on the parking lot can move up. One action finished means one parking-lot item can enter. If something becomes yellow or red, apply Chapter 2.

> **One family's story**
>
> Dana's list says "fix Dad's medications," and for three weeks nothing happens. When she shrinks it to "photograph every bottle on Sunday," she gets it done in fifteen minutes and finds that Frank uses three pharmacies and has two prescriptions for nearly the same blood pressure drug. After one pharmacist review, he's down to a single pharmacy and four fewer pills a day.

### 4. Exactly what to say

**To your parent, proposing a start:** "I don't want to change everything. There are three things on my list: the medications, the bathroom, and the bills. Which one would feel least intrusive to start with?"

**To a sibling, assigning an owner:** "Can you own the insurance piece? Call Mom's plan and find out whether she needs referrals or prior authorization. Can you finish by Friday and text me what you learn?"

**To the doctor's office, before the visit:** "I'm bringing my father Thursday. We have three concerns: \[one, two, three\]. Could you let the doctor know ahead of time, and tell me if we should book a longer visit?"

**In the exam room, before leaving:** "Let me make sure I have this right: \[repeat the plan\]. What should we watch for, and when should we call?"

**To a referral desk:** "I'm calling to confirm you received the referral for \[name, birthdate\] from Dr. \[X\] on \[date\]. What's the first available appointment, and will you send your notes back to Dr. \[X\]?"

**To anyone pressing you to do more:** "That's on the parking lot until \[date\]. We're finishing three things first."

## Chapter 5: How Do I Bring This Up Without Starting a Fight?

*Days 15–21 of your 30-day plan*

### 1. The frustration

*"Every conversation ends with 'I'm fine,' 'It's none of your business,' or both."*

You rehearsed in the car. "Mom, I've been thinking..." Before you finish, she says "I'm fine," and the kitchen gets colder. You push. She says, "It's none of your business." Then you hear yourself say, "Well, somebody has to." Ten minutes later you're both angry, nothing has changed, and you've become the villain in her version of the story.

Most of the time, the trouble is the timing, the order you say things in, and a few phrases that land as attacks. Those are fixable.

### 2. What may be happening

**What you say is not what they hear.** You say "I'm worried." They hear "You're losing control." In AARP's 2024 Home and Community Preferences Survey, 75% of adults 50 and older said they wish to remain in their current homes, so every offer of help can sound like the first step toward leaving. Driving carries the same weight.

**Pushing backfires.** Motivational interviewing calls the urge to fix someone the "righting reflex." When you argue for change, an ambivalent person tends to take up and defend the opposite side. The alternative is to ask permission before giving advice.

**Sometimes it isn't stubbornness.** In dementia, people may lack awareness of their illness. When short-term memory fails, a person may not remember that they can't remember. The refusal is a symptom, not intentional denial. Facts won't win that argument. A method called LEAP (Listen, Empathize, Agree, Partner) is a non-confrontational approach designed to build trust.

**Tone matters more than you'd think.** Baby-talk-style "elderspeak" can backfire. In a small 2021 hospital study published in Innovation in Aging, a 15-percentage-point drop in staff elderspeak was linked to 62% lower odds that a person with dementia would reject care. The study involved just 16 patients and 53 staff members at one hospital, so treat it as a signal, not proof.

**You may not be the right messenger.** Sometimes the best move is to hand the message to someone your parent trusts, such as their doctor.

### 3. What to do now

**1. Start early, small, and private.** Pick a calm time, not a holiday dinner and not when either of you is tired. Take one topic. The Alzheimer's Association advises having the first conversation as soon as possible, and notes that the first attempt may not succeed, so write down what happened and what to change. Waiting is common. In a 2021 survey for the Alzheimer's Association and the Ad Council, only 44% of Americans said they would talk to a loved one right away about seeing a doctor after noticing cognitive decline.

**2. Use a four-part formula:** *your intention, one specific observation, a permission question, a small choice.* ("I want you to stay in this house. I noticed the stairs light is out. Can I fix that this week, or would you rather call the electrician?")

**3. Use the LEAP loop.** Listen without interrupting. Empathize ("That would frustrate me too"). Agree on a goal ("We both want you home"). Then partner on a step.

**4. Borrow authority.** Ask the doctor to raise the issue, or suggest an assessment instead of a verdict.

**5. When they refuse, don't escalate.** Say you'll revisit it, set a date, and log what happened. Refusal on a green topic can wait. A red flag from Chapter 2 can't. If safety is at stake, you may need to act through a doctor, Adult Protective Services, or the DMV even without agreement.

**6. Topic by topic:**

- **Driving.** Start before it's a problem, and don't open with scary risk statistics. Try a starter like "Driving isn't what it used to be." Line up rides first. A 2016 pooled analysis of five studies found that people who stopped driving had nearly twice the odds of depressive symptoms, though the studies couldn't show that stopping caused the depression.
- **Memory.** Frame it as a checkup: "Let's see if the doctor can help us figure out what's going on," and offer to go along. The Alzheimer's Association's free 24/7 Helpline is 800-272-3900.
- **Money.** Ask about safeguards, not amounts. Chapter 3's trusted-contact idea is a good first ask.
- **Home safety.** Tie every suggestion to staying home.

**7. Talk with doctors without speaking over your parent.** Plan together beforehand. Decide what issues to raise, who will raise them, and whether either of you wants time alone with the provider. Write the doctor a note in advance, let your parent and the doctor lead, and book a separate call or visit if you have more to say. Know that some patients won't speak honestly about sensitive subjects in front of a family member, and that some doctors will check partway through whether the patient is comfortable with you in the room. From Chapter 3, nothing stops you from telling a provider what you've noticed, even if they can't share anything back.

**8. Choose language that preserves dignity.**

| Instead of | Try |
| --- | --- |
| "You need to..." / "You can't..." | "Would you be open to...?" / "I'm worried about..." |
| "You always..." / "You never..." | One specific, recent example |
| "I'm taking over." | "I'd like to help with X. You stay in charge of Y." |
| "Remember when I told you?" | Just say it again, calmly. Don't try to jog the memory. |
| "That's not true!" / "Don't be silly." | "That sounds upsetting. Let's look together." |
| "Good job, sweetie!" | Adult tone. Talking down can feel patronizing and infantilizing. |

With accusations, ask yourself whether correcting is worth it. If your parent keeps accusing someone of taking a wallet, keep it in an easy-to-remember spot so you can show it's safe.

> **One family's story**
>
> At Thanksgiving, in front of everyone, Marcus tells Ruth she shouldn't be driving anymore. She leaves the table and doesn't speak to him for two days. In January, he tries again, privately and on the phone. He asks permission to bring it up, mentions the new dent in her car, and suggests a driving evaluation so she can prove him wrong. She agrees, because the decision is still hers.

### 4. Exactly what to say

Use this skeleton for any conversation:

**"I \[care about / want\] \_\_\_. I've noticed \_\_\_. Would you be open to \_\_\_? Or would you rather \_\_\_?"**

*Worked example (money):* "I want you to keep control of your money. I noticed a few bills went unopened. Would you be open to adding me as a trusted contact at the bank, so they call me if something looks like a scam? I wouldn't see your accounts. Or would you rather pick someone else?"

*Worked example (driving):* "I want you to keep your independence. I got nervous on Tuesday at the turn by the church. Would you be open to a driving evaluation, or would you rather start with Dr. Lee's opinion?"

The full toolkit is next.

### 5. Tool: Twenty-Five Difficult-Conversation Scripts

**Opening**

**1. First conversation:** "Mom, I'm not here to take anything over. I've noticed a few things, and I'd rather ask you than guess. Is now a good time?"

**2. Bad timing:** "It sounds like this isn't a good moment. Can we try Sunday after lunch?"

**3. After a blowup:** "I handled that badly. I care about you and I got pushy. Can we start over?"

**When they refuse**

**4. "I'm fine."** "I believe you want to be fine. I'd just feel better if we checked. What would be the easiest first step?"

**5. "It's none of your business."** "You're right that it's your life. I love you, so I worry. Can you tell me what part feels intrusive?"

**6. "I don't need help."** "I'm not saying you do. Could I help with one small thing, just so I stop worrying?"

**7. "Stop treating me like a child."** "You're right, and I'm sorry. How would you want me to bring things up?"

**Driving**

**8.** "Driving isn't what it used to be. Do you ever feel it's harder than it was?"

**11.** "I've noticed we've both had some trouble remembering things lately. Let's see if the doctor can help us figure out what's going on."

**17.** "I want you to stay in this house. What would make it feel safer to you?"

**19. Anger:** "I can see how upset you are. I'm going to step out for ten minutes and come back."

**20. "You're stealing from me":** "That sounds frightening. Let's look for it together." *(Don't argue.)*

**21. Repetition:** Answer the question again, in the same calm tone as the first time. Add a written note or calendar entry if it helps.

**22. Suspicion of you or a sibling:** "You have every right to ask questions. Shall we call the bank together and check?"

**Doctors**

**23. Before the visit:** "Is there anything you want me to bring up? Anything you'd rather say yourself?"

**24. In the room:** "Mom, you tell the doctor how you've been feeling, and then I'll add what I've noticed. Is that okay?"

**25. Privately, afterward:** "I want to share a concern without upsetting my mother. Could I send a note, or schedule a call?"

### 6. When professional help is appropriate

- **The doctor:** ask them to raise memory, driving, or medication concerns. Many parents will take it more seriously from a doctor than from a child.
- **Alzheimer's Association Helpline:** call the free 24/7 line, 800-272-3900, for scripts tailored to your situation.
- **Driving:** a doctor or occupational therapist can assess. If your parent refuses and is a danger, you can contact your local DMV, and some states accept anonymous reports while others don't.
- **Family conflict:** a geriatric care manager, social worker, or mediator can serve as the neutral voice.
- **Safety emergencies:** use Chapter 2. Suicidal talk means 988 or 911. Suspected abuse or exploitation means Adult Protective Services.
- **You:** if conversations leave you shaking or resentful, a counselor or caregiver group can help.

Pick one script and one topic this week. If it goes badly, write down what happened and try again.

## Chapter 6: They Can Still Make Decisions—So When Should I Step In?

*Days 22–25 of your 30-day plan*

### 1. The frustration

*"My parent is still independent in many ways, but some decisions are becoming risky."*

Your mother still runs her book club and does her own taxes. She also has two bottles of the same pill on the counter, and last week she mentioned "a very nice man from the government" who needs her to buy gift cards. You don't have clear grounds to step in. Step in too early and you take her dignity. Wait too long and she may lose her savings, her health, or her license to someone else's mistake, or her own.

Nobody can tell you exactly where the line is. You can work it out one decision at a time, and the rest of this chapter shows how.

### 2. What may be happening

**The starting point is respect.** Capacity is decision-specific. Adults are presumed to have it, and an unwise choice is not proof it's missing. A patient with capacity can make decisions that lead to illness, discomfort, danger, or even death, and relatives often need education and support when that happens. So the question isn't "Is Mom competent?" It's "Can she understand, weigh, and communicate *this* decision?"

**Money often shows trouble first.** A 2020 study in JAMA Internal Medicine of about 81,000 Medicare beneficiaries 65 and older living alone found that people who later developed dementia were more likely to miss payments on credit accounts beginning about six years before diagnosis, and more likely to have subprime credit scores starting about two and a half years before. The differences were small, so one late bill proves nothing. Look for patterns.

**Scam losses are growing.** The FBI's 2025 figures show adults 60 and older reported $7.7 billion in losses, about a 60% increase from 2024. In August 2025 the FTC reported that, from 2020 to 2024, reports of losses of $10,000 or more by adults 60 and older to impersonators more than quadrupled. Payment method is a tell. The FTC has reported gift cards, bank transfers, and Bitcoin ATMs among the ways impersonation scammers get paid.

**Suspicion isn't always dementia.** Paranoia in older adults can come from medication side effects, hearing and vision problems, delirium, and dementia, and many causes are treatable. If it comes on suddenly, call for an urgent appointment that includes evaluation for a new illness or medication side effect.

**Medication errors are easy to miss.** Duplicates are easy to create when several doctors and pharmacies are involved, because the same drug can go by both a brand name and a generic name.

### 3. What to do now

**Use the ladder, starting at the bottom.** State laws generally allow guardianship only as a last resort and require consideration of less restrictive alternatives, and your approach should follow the same logic.

1. **Observe and log** (details below).
2. **Talk and offer.** Use Chapter 5's scripts.
3. **Add safeguards that leave decisions with your parent.** Examples are a bank trusted contact, one pharmacy, and a "call me first" rule.
4. **Make support formal while capacity remains.** Supported decision-making lets people keep making their own decisions with support from trusted individuals they choose. Powers of attorney (Chapter 3) work the same way.
5. **Get a professional assessment** for a specific decision.
6. **Go to court** only when everything else has failed, and only with an elder law attorney.

**Topic playbooks**

| Concern | Signs | Gentle first step | Stronger step |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Money and scams** | Secret phone calls, urgent "government" demands, gift cards, wire or crypto requests, unopened bills, unusual purchases | "Call me before sending money to anyone who contacted you first." Add a trusted contact. | Bank fraud department, FTC report at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, or the National Elder Fraud Hotline (1-833-372-8311). The FTC says it will never demand money, make threats, or tell you to transfer money. |
| **Driving** | Unexplained dents and scrapes, trouble following signals, needing help with familiar routes | Ride along, then use Chapter 5's scripts | A driver rehabilitation specialist can test driving on and off the road and help decide when to stop. |
| **Medications** | Duplicates, missed doses, confusion about pills | Brown-bag review (Chapter 3) | One pharmacy and synchronized refills; blister packaging |
| **Suspicion** | New accusations, fear of intruders | Rule out medical causes first. Don't argue. | Doctor visit within days, sooner if sudden |

For any sudden, new suspicion, treat it as a Chapter 2 yellow. If there's violence, weapons, or a threat to anyone's safety, call 911.

**Document without building a case.** Your log (Chapter 2) exists to help professionals see patterns, not to prove your parent is incompetent.

- Record facts, dates, and what you saw, not labels like "paranoid."
- Include what's going *well*.
- Tell your parent: "I'm keeping a list of things to ask the doctor. Want to add anything?"
- Don't record conversations secretly. Federal law requires only one party's consent, but about 11 states generally require everyone's consent, so check your state's law. Secret recording also costs trust.
- Don't open mail, log into accounts, or move money without permission or legal authority.
- Share the log with doctors and your care team, not as ammunition in sibling disputes.

> **One family's story**
>
> Ruth wires $4,000 to a caller claiming to be her grandson in jail. She's embarrassed, and she insists she can still manage her own money. Marcus decides she's right: one bad decision doesn't mean she can't make decisions. Instead of taking over her accounts, he asks her to name him as a trusted contact at her bank and to set an alert for any withdrawal over $500. She keeps control, and when the next scam call comes, the bank catches it.

### 4. Exactly what to say

**Money:** "I'm not asking to see your accounts. I'd like us to have a rule: before you send money or gift cards to anyone who contacted you first, you call me. I won't say no. I just want a second set of ears."

**Bills:** "Would you let me be your backup? You'd keep paying them. The company would send me a copy, so nothing slips if you're ever sick."

**Medications:** "Could we ask the pharmacy to pack everything in a calendar card? Then you don't have to sort pills every week."

**Suspicion about a missing item:** "That sounds upsetting. Let's look together." *(Find it. Don't argue.)*

**Suspicion about someone entering the house:** "That sounds frightening. I want you to feel safe. Could Dr. Lee check whether a medicine or an infection could be making nights harder?"

**When they say "I'm still independent":** "You are, and I want to keep it that way. These two safeguards are so you can keep deciding."

**To a sibling who sees your log as a case:** "I'm keeping a log so Dr. Lee has facts. It isn't to prove anything about Mom."

### 6. When professional help is appropriate

- **The doctor:** start here to rule out medical causes. If cognitive impairment is suspected, Medicare covers a separate visit for a more detailed cognitive assessment and care plan. Medicare's description of that visit includes an evaluation of safety, including driving, and an assessment of the caregiver's needs and role.

## Chapter 7: But Our Family Situation Is Different

*Days 26–28 of your 30-day plan*

### 1. The frustration

*"Most advice assumes we live nearby, have money, agree with one another, and have a cooperative parent. We don't."*

The advice reads fine until you try to use it. "Hold a family meeting," but your brother hasn't spoken to your sister in six years. "Visit weekly," but you live nine hours away. "Hire an aide," but a quick search shows the going rate, and you do the math. "Talk to your mom," but she hangs up on you.

Every article quietly begins with the same sentence: *If your parent is cooperative and you live close and the family gets along...* You're not that family. Almost nobody is. So the plan has to start from the family you actually have.

### 2. What may be happening

Generic advice fits an average family that doesn't exist. Your situation differs along five dials, and the combination changes everything: **distance, your parent's cooperation, how many hands you have, whether the issue is physical or cognitive, and money.**

Caregiving takes a toll. In AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving's 2025 report, 63 million Americans provided care, nearly one in four of them 40 or more hours a week, and half of employed caregivers reported work disruptions. One in five caregivers reported fair or poor health. Your situation shapes how much of that falls on you. AARP's 2016 survey of caregiver costs found that long-distance caregivers had the highest out-of-pocket costs ($11,923 a year), and that caring for someone with dementia cost far more ($10,697 versus $5,758).

So aim for the **minimum workable level of support**, not the *ideal* plan: the smallest amount of help that keeps your parent safe and as independent as they want to be, and that your family can sustain for at least a year. A plan that burns you out in three months isn't a plan.

- **Money:** out-of-pocket costs are outrunning your budget.
- **Trend:** your parent keeps declining despite your effort, or has been hospitalized more than once.

### 4. Exactly what to say

**Opening a sibling meeting:** "I'm not asking for equal. I'm asking for specific. Here are six tasks. Which would you take?"

**To a far-away sibling:** "I know you can't be here. The most useful thing would be to own the insurance calls and bills, and to cover me one weekend a quarter."

**To a sibling in denial:** "I'm not trying to make you feel guilty. I'd like you to hear what the doctor said. Would you join the call Thursday?"

**To your parent, about outside help:** "This isn't replacing family. It's so I can keep being your daughter instead of your full-time aide. Can we try it for 30 days?"

**To a neighbor:** "Would you be one of three people I can call if something seems off? I'd only ask for a quick look."

**To an agency:** "Are your caregivers employees, and are they background-checked? What's the minimum shift? What are your rates for nights and weekends?"

**To yourself:** "Good enough and sustainable beats perfect and short-lived."

## Chapter 8: Where Is the Insurance Card—and Who Paid That Bill?

*Days 29–30 of your 30-day plan*

### 1. The frustration

It's 6:40 p.m. and the ER nurse asks for the insurance card, the medication list, and the name of your mother's cardiologist. You're standing in her kitchen, opening drawers. The card isn't in her wallet. The medication list is a photo on your sister's phone. The cardiologist's name is on a business card that is "in the blue folder," which is not in the blue folder.

Neither of you did anything wrong. Information that worked fine while one person carried it in their head has to be findable by someone else, under pressure. About 63 million Americans, nearly one in four adults, provided care for a family member or friend in 2025, according to AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving's *Caregiving in the US 2025*. Many learn this lesson mid-crisis. You're going to learn it now.

### 2. What may be happening

Three things are usually going on at once.

- **The records are scattered by design.** Insurers mail paper, doctors use portals, banks use apps, and each has its own login. Your parent may know exactly where everything is. That knowledge just isn't written down.
- **Nobody has been named.** Without authorization paperwork, offices can't talk to you. Without a central place, you can't hand the job to anyone else.
- **The money is mixed.** The first time you put a pharmacy bill on your own card "just this once," you start a record-keeping problem. It affects your parent's taxes, your own budget, and, later, your siblings' trust.

One caution before you gather everything in one place. The information that rescues you in an ER is also what a scammer wants. Medicare cards no longer carry a Social Security number; they carry a randomly generated Medicare Beneficiary Identifier. It still shouldn't be photographed and texted around.

### 3. What to do now

**Day 29: build the core (about two hours).**

1. **Print the binder** and start with page 2, the Emergency Quick Sheet. Print two copies: one for the fridge, one for your bag.
2. **Fill pages 3 through 5 from the real objects**, not from memory. Use the pill bottles for medications, the cards for insurance (copy front and back), and the business cards or portal pages for doctors.
3. **Use page 6 to record where the legal documents are.** Don't copy their contents. Keep copies in the binder and put the originals in a fireproof safe or with the attorney. A safe deposit box can be a poor home for a power of attorney. An agent may need the original to get into the box, and banks can restrict access after a death. Rules vary by state, so ask an elder law attorney.

**Day 30: money, access, routines.**

4. **Start the expense log (page 8) today.** The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's guidance for agents under a power of attorney comes down to four duties: act only in your parent's interest, manage their money carefully, keep your money separate from theirs, and keep good records. Even without a power of attorney, the habit protects you. It also matters at tax time. If your parent is your dependent, you may be able to deduct, if you itemize, the part of the medical costs you paid that exceeds 7.5% of your adjusted gross income (IRS Publication 502).
5. **Fill the Accounts and Digital Access Index (page 10) as a map, not a vault.** Write where each login is kept and who can reach it, never the login itself. Put the actual passwords in a password manager with an emergency-access feature. Bitwarden (a paid Premium feature), LastPass, and Keeper offer a trusted contact who can request access after a waiting period you set. As of this writing, 1Password's support pages describe a printed Emergency Kit instead of an emergency-access feature. These features change, so check each company's current plan details.
6. **Put a 10-minute monthly appointment on your calendar** for page 12. Part of that appointment is reading the Medicare Summary Notice. Original Medicare mails one every three months when it was billed, or monthly if your parent signs up for electronic notices. Look for any service your parent didn't receive. You can report suspicious billing to 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227) or the Senior Medicare Patrol (1-877-808-2468).

- Paramedics and the ER need page 2.

- Everyone else gets last-4-digit references.

## Chapter 9: I Don't Have Time to Read a Textbook

*Day 30, and every week after*

**The two-minute version.** If you read nothing else:

1. Fill in **one dashboard a week**. It takes ten minutes.
2. Give every task **one owner**, with a name and a date.
3. Fill in the **prep sheet** before every appointment.
4. When something is off, use the **decision trees**. Red means call 911, yellow means call the doctor today, and green means write it down.
5. Write the **handoff sheet** while things are calm, not on the worst day.

### 1. The frustration

You're in your car at lunch. The pharmacy has called, your sister has texted, the school has emailed, and you're scanning the index of a 300-page caregiving book for "what to do when Dad stops eating." You don't have time for a chapter. You need a page.

The load is real. In *Caregiving in the US 2025*, AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving found that people caring for an adult 50 or older spend an average of 26 hours a week on it, up from 22 in 2020. Six in 10 family caregivers are employed, and half of those report work disruptions. Just over 20% have had any formal training on medical or nursing tasks, though over half manage them. You don't need more information. You need less of it, in the right place.

### 2. What may be happening

- **Books are organized by topic. Your problems arrive by urgency.** The answer exists, but you can't find it fast enough to use it.
- **The system is you.** Appointments, refill dates, and who promised what live in one head. That works until it doesn't.
- **You remake the same decisions every time.** Without rules, "Should I call the doctor?" gets decided from scratch at 9 p.m. each time.

What helps is a few pages that hold the information for you.

### 3. What to do now

**The ten-minute weekly review** (Dashboard, page 1). Pick a fixed time, like Sunday evening. Spend two minutes looking back (fill in *What changed?*), three looking ahead (appointments, refills), three assigning (every open task gets one owner), and two sharing (send a photo of the page to your backup and siblings).

**Appointment prep on one sheet** (page 2). The National Institute on Aging suggests ranking your concerns and planning on three or four, because that's what a visit can realistically cover. Bring the medicines (or the bottles), insurance cards, and other doctors' names. Tell anyone going with you what you want from the visit, and ask your parent whether they'd like time alone with the doctor. Before you leave, repeat the plan back in your own words.

**Shared calendar and task ownership** (page 3). Create one calendar named for your parent, and invite siblings and your backup person. Put every appointment on it with who is driving. Pick one place for messages and one for documents. Free tools can help, such as the care calendar at Lotsa Helping Hands. The real rule is on the page: whoever claims a task owns it until they hand it off out loud.

**Tracking changes without writing a diary** (Dashboard, *What changed?*). Check Better, Same, or Worse for each area, and add one line only when something is worse. Save full entries for yellow and red events (incident log, binder page 11). Log the change, not the day.

**Simple decision trees** (pages 4 and 5). There are four:

- Something is different today.
- I can't reach my parent.
- A medicine problem.
- "I don't need help."

Stroke signs (BE-FAST: sudden trouble with Balance, Eyes, Face, Arms, or Speech) are a 911 call, even if the symptoms pass, and you should note when they started (American Stroke Association). When you're unsure, go one color up.

**An emergency handoff another person can understand** (page 6). Hospitals use structured handoffs for the same reason. In a 2014 *New England Journal of Medicine* study of structured handoffs (called I-PASS) across nine pediatric hospital residency programs, medical errors fell 23% and preventable adverse events fell 30%. Your parent's care isn't a hospital ward, but the same idea applies: use the same headings every time, and have the receiver say it back. The handoff sheet adapts the idea to five parts:

1. How stable things are
2. A short summary
3. A to-do list
4. If this happens, do this
5. A read-back

> **One family's story**
>
> Marcus and his sister used to talk about their mother in long, anxious phone calls that ended without a plan. Now they hold a 20-minute call every Sunday evening with a one-page dashboard: appointments, medication changes, new concerns, and who's doing what. Most calls end with three decisions and one owner for each.

### 4. Exactly what to say

**To your siblings, setting up the weekly update:** "Every Sunday I'll send a photo of a one-page dashboard. Read it, ask questions, and claim a task if you want one. If it's urgent, I'll call."

**To a sibling, about ownership:** "Which of these do you want to own? I'd rather you own one thing completely than help a little with everything."

**To the doctor, at the start of the visit:** "We have three things for today. The most important is that she's been dizzy when she stands up."

**To your parent, about the handoff:** "I'm writing down what a good day looks like for you, so if I'm ever away, whoever helps can keep things normal. What matters most about your mornings?"

**To the person covering for you:** "Here's the sheet. Read it back to me in your own words, and tell me what's unclear."

## Chapter 10: What Happens When This Gets Worse?

*Days 31–90, and beyond*

**The two-minute version.**

1. **Stable** means the system works without you holding it together. Score it once a month.
2. Know your **triggers**. One trigger: tell your backup. Two in a month: reassess.
3. Add help **one step at a time**, not all at once.
4. In a hospital, ask on day one: **"Inpatient or observation?"**
5. Agree now on **the signs**. Decide the move later.
6. Put **your own** health, job, and limits on paper.

### 1. The frustration

It's Sunday night, and for the first time in a month nothing is on fire. You can't relax. Every phone call feels like the other shoe about to drop.

There's a reason for that. About one in four adults 65 and older reports a fall each year, according to the CDC, and the age-adjusted fall death rate for that group rose 21% from 2018 to 2024. This chapter starts where the 30-day plan ends. You can't prevent the next crisis, but you can make it smaller and less frightening.

### 2. What may be happening

- **The first 30 days fixed the emergency, not the direction.** Stability now means the plan is holding, not that things have stopped changing.
- **Change usually arrives as a step, not a slope.** A fall, a hospital stay, or a spouse's death can change everything in a day.
- **Families decide in the crisis.** Deciding ahead can feel like betraying a parent. So the first conversation happens in a hospital hallway.

The fix is to decide the signs now and the move later.

### 3. What to do now

**What stability looks like (Day 30).** Run the eight-point Stability Check (page 1). Examples: medicines taken as planned, no falls or ER visits this month, every ride has an owner, a backup could run a day from the handoff sheet, and you're keeping your own appointments. A score of 7 to 8 is stable. A score of 4 or lower means re-running the Chapter 3 assessment this week.

**Changes that trigger a new assessment.** The page lists ten, including a hospital stay or fall, new confusion, a bill or scam problem, a helper who quits, and you being exhausted most days. One trigger: update the dashboard and tell your backup. Two in a month: re-run Chapter 3, then the Chapter 7 Care Path Builder.

**Increasing help at home.** Climb a step only when a trigger says so: tools, then family on a schedule, then a few paid hours, daily help, around-the-clock care, and finally a move. CareScout's 2025 median is $35 an hour. Ten hours a week is about $18,200 a year. Medicare covers part-time skilled home health if your parent is homebound and a doctor orders it. It doesn't cover around-the-clock care, home-delivered meals, or personal care as the only need. If your parent has dementia and Original Medicare, ask about Medicare's GUIDE program, which includes a care navigator, a 24/7 helpline, and up to $2,500 a year in respite. Not every area has a participating provider.

**Hospital and rehab transitions.** Ask on day one whether your parent is an inpatient or in observation. Observation days don't count toward the three inpatient days Medicare requires for skilled nursing facility coverage. Hospitals should give an "Important Message from Medicare" within two days of admission. If you believe a discharge is too soon, you can request a fast appeal, no later than the planned discharge day. In 2026, Medicare's skilled nursing facility coverage costs you $0 for days 1–20 (after the $1,736 Part A deductible, if you haven't already paid it for that stay), then $217 a day for days 21–100. In older federal data from 2010, nearly one in five Medicare patients discharged from a hospital were readmitted within 30 days, and rates vary by hospital, so use the roadmap's first-72-hours list.

**When living alone may no longer be safe.** Page 6 has a ten-item check. Two or three items: step up help and ask the doctor for an assessment. Four or more, or any one of these, meet this week: can't get up after a fall, wandering, or a fire risk.

**Comparing your options.**

| Option | Typical cost (national medians; CareScout 2025, A Place for Mom 2026) | Who may pay |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Home care | $34–$35 an hour | Private pay, insurance; Medicare for part-time skilled care only |
| Assisted living | About $5,400–$6,200 a month | Private pay, insurance; Medicaid varies by state |
| Memory care | About $6,690 a month | Same; Medicare doesn't cover custodial care |
| Nursing home | $315 a day semi-private ($114,975 a year) | Medicare for short rehab; Medicaid if eligible |
| Family care | About 26 hours a week; about $7,200 a year out of pocket | You |

**Preparing without deciding too soon.** Tour two places and collect fee schedules and contracts, but pay no deposits. Don't sell the house or retitle assets without an elder law attorney. Don't promise "never a facility" or "always home."

**Protecting your health, work, and family.** The Alzheimer's Association reported in November 2025 that 59% of dementia family caregivers rate their emotional stress as high or very high. The federal Family and Medical Leave Act (FMLA) allows eligible employees up to 12 workweeks of unpaid, job-protected leave in a 12-month period to care for a parent with a serious health condition, in blocks or on a reduced schedule. To qualify, you generally need 12 months and 1,250 hours with an employer that has 50 or more employees within 75 miles of your worksite. Schedule respite, and put your limits in writing on page 7.

**Building the 30-, 60-, and 90-day plan.** Days 1–30: stabilize and test. Days 31–60: strengthen (trial help, tours, attorney, triggers). Days 61–90: reassess and decide at a second family meeting.

> **One family's story**
>
> Carmen falls and breaks her hip. In the hospital, the sisters are told she'll go to rehab, but nobody says what happens after that. They ask to meet the discharge planner early, tour two rehab options, and use the 90-day plan to decide, together with Carmen, how much help she'll need when she comes home.

### 4. Exactly what to say

**To the hospital:** "Is my mother admitted as an inpatient or in observation? When was that decided? I'd like to speak with the case manager today about discharge."

**If discharge feels too soon:** "I don't think she's safe to go home yet, because she can't get to the bathroom alone. I'd like her Important Message from Medicare and instructions for a fast appeal."

**To your parent, about more help:** "I'm not trying to take over. I'm trying to keep you home. Let's try a few hours a week for a month and see how it goes."

**To your siblings:** "Let's agree now on what would make us change the plan, so we're not arguing in a hospital hallway."

**To your employer:** "I'm caring for a parent with a serious health condition. Can we talk about FMLA or a flexible schedule?"

**To your family, about respite:** "I need one weekend off every quarter. Who can cover?"
