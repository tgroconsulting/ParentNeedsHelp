# Chapter 7: But Our Family Situation Is Different

*Days 26–28 of your 30-day plan*

## 1. The frustration

*"Most advice assumes we live nearby, have money, agree with one another, and have a cooperative parent. We don't."*

The advice reads fine until you try to use it. "Hold a family meeting," but your brother hasn't spoken to your sister in six years. "Visit weekly," but you live nine hours away. "Hire an aide," but a quick search shows the going rate, and you do the math. "Talk to your mom," but she hangs up on you.

Every article quietly begins with the same sentence: *If your parent is cooperative and you live close and the family gets along...* You're not that family. Almost nobody is. So the plan has to start from the family you actually have.

## 2. What may be happening

Generic advice fits an average family that doesn't exist. Your situation differs along five dials, and the combination changes everything: **distance, your parent's cooperation, how many hands you have, whether the issue is physical or cognitive, and money.**

Caregiving takes a toll. In AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving's 2025 report, 63 million Americans provided care, nearly one in four of them 40 or more hours a week, and half of employed caregivers reported work disruptions. One in five caregivers reported fair or poor health. Your situation shapes how much of that falls on you. AARP's 2016 survey of caregiver costs found that long-distance caregivers had the highest out-of-pocket costs ($11,923 a year), and that caring for someone with dementia cost far more ($10,697 versus $5,758).

So aim for the **minimum workable level of support**, not the *ideal* plan: the smallest amount of help that keeps your parent safe and as independent as they want to be, and that your family can sustain for at least a year. A plan that burns you out in three months isn't a plan.

**For limited finances, work this list in order:**

1. **Eldercare Locator (1-800-677-1116)** to reach your Area Agency on Aging. Through the National Family Caregiver Support Program, caregivers can get information, counseling, support groups, training, respite care, and limited supplemental services. What's offered, and who qualifies, varies by area.
2. **Medicaid.** In many states, Medicaid pays for non-medical home care and in-home supports for people who qualify. It has income and asset limits and may involve wait lists, so apply early.
3. **PACE**, a Medicare and Medicaid program for people 55 and older who need nursing-home-level care but can live safely in the community with its support. It's offered only in some states and areas.
4. **Veterans benefits**, if your parent served. The VA offers homemaker and home health aide care, and Aid & Attendance payments on top of a VA pension for qualified veterans.
5. **Be careful with assets.** In most states, Medicaid reviews transfers made in the five years before you apply and can impose a penalty period. Informal gifts and unwritten loans to family can cause trouble. See an elder law attorney first.

**Step 3: Choose your level.** These are national medians from CareScout's 2025 Cost of Care Survey. Local costs vary, and the weekly and yearly figures at Levels 2 through 4 are calculated from them.

| Level | What it looks like | Rough cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | Calls, a neighbor who checks in | $0 |
| 1 | Rides, delivered meals, pill organizer, emergency alert, mail check | Low |
| 2 | Part-time paid help. The national median for a non-medical caregiver is $35 an hour, so 4 hours a week is about $7,280 a year and 10 hours about $18,200. Adult day health care is $95 a day, so two days a week is about $9,880. | $7,000 to $20,000+/yr |
| 3 | Daily help, about 44 hours a week | About $80,080 a year |
| 4 | Assisted living | $6,200 a month ($74,400 a year) |
| 5 | Nursing home or skilled care | $315 a day for a semi-private room ($114,975 a year); private duty nursing at home is $90 an hour |

Pick the *lowest* level that passes three tests: **Is it safe? Can we sustain it for a year? Will my parent accept it?** Re-test every 90 days and after any red or yellow event. Adult day programs deserve a look because they remain one of the more affordable options while providing supervision, engagement, and structured daytime support.

**Step 4: Build a local backup network, even from far away.**

- **Eyes:** a neighbor, and Carrier Alert, a free service where letter carriers watch for mail piling up and alert a local agency, which calls your parent and then their emergency contact. It isn't offered everywhere, so ask the post office or the local letter carriers' branch.
- **Hands:** someone within an hour. Consider giving a trusted person a key or garage code, and keep a medical conditions and medication list on the fridge.
- **Rides and errands:** faith community, volunteers, Area Agency on Aging programs.
- **Professionals:** the doctor's office contact, the pharmacist, a care manager.
- **Emergency:** who has the Grab Sheet from Chapter 2.

**Step 5: Know when free family care is no longer enough.** Watch for these tripwires:

- **Safety:** your parent needs supervision at night, wanders, falls with no one around, or needs lifting or medical care you aren't trained for.
- **You:** you're providing 40 or more hours on top of a job, missing your own medical care, snapping at your parent, or dreading each day.
- **Money:** out-of-pocket costs are outrunning your budget.
- **Trend:** your parent keeps declining despite your effort, or has been hospitalized more than once.

Three or more means act this week. Start with respite. The ARCH National Respite Locator (archrespite.org) helps you find programs by location. Then add paid hours.

> **One family's story**
>
> Elena is worn out and resents Sofia, who sends money but rarely visits. Sofia feels shut out of decisions. During a tense video call, they stop arguing about what's fair and list what each of them is actually good at. Sofia takes over the insurance calls, the bills, and researching options from across the country. She also pays for a home aide two afternoons a week, so Elena gets regular time off.

## 4. Exactly what to say

**Opening a sibling meeting:** "I'm not asking for equal. I'm asking for specific. Here are six tasks. Which would you take?"

**To a far-away sibling:** "I know you can't be here. The most useful thing would be to own the insurance calls and bills, and to cover me one weekend a quarter."

**To a sibling in denial:** "I'm not trying to make you feel guilty. I'd like you to hear what the doctor said. Would you join the call Thursday?"

**To your parent, about outside help:** "This isn't replacing family. It's so I can keep being your daughter instead of your full-time aide. Can we try it for 30 days?"

**To a neighbor:** "Would you be one of three people I can call if something seems off? I'd only ask for a quick look."

**To an agency:** "Are your caregivers employees, and are they background-checked? What's the minimum shift? What are your rates for nights and weekends?"

**To yourself:** "Good enough and sustainable beats perfect and short-lived."

## 6. When professional help is appropriate

- **A geriatric care manager:** especially if you're far away. They're typically nurses or social workers who assess needs and coordinate local services, and they can be expensive but valuable.
- **An elder law attorney:** before gifts, transfers, or paying family members for care.
- **Your Area Agency on Aging:** for respite, programs, and benefits screening.
- **A mediator, social worker, or counselor:** when siblings can't talk without a fight.
- **A hospital discharge planner or social worker:** after any hospital stay.
- **Adult Protective Services:** if your parent is being neglected or exploited.
- **A counselor or support group for you.**

Choose your level, put the review date on the calendar, and call the Area Agency on Aging if you've checked three tripwires.
