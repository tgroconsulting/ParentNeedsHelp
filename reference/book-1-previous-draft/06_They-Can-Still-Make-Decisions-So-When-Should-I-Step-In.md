# Chapter 6: They Can Still Make Decisions—So When Should I Step In?

*Days 22–25 of your 30-day plan*

## 1. The frustration

*"My parent is still independent in many ways, but some decisions are becoming risky."*

Your mother still runs her book club and does her own taxes. She also has two bottles of the same pill on the counter, and last week she mentioned "a very nice man from the government" who needs her to buy gift cards. You don't have clear grounds to step in. Step in too early and you take her dignity. Wait too long and she may lose her savings, her health, or her license to someone else's mistake, or her own.

Nobody can tell you exactly where the line is. You can work it out one decision at a time, and the rest of this chapter shows how.

## 2. What may be happening

**The starting point is respect.** Capacity is decision-specific. Adults are presumed to have it, and an unwise choice is not proof it's missing. A patient with capacity can make decisions that lead to illness, discomfort, danger, or even death, and relatives often need education and support when that happens. So the question isn't "Is Mom competent?" It's "Can she understand, weigh, and communicate *this* decision?"

**Money often shows trouble first.** A 2020 study in JAMA Internal Medicine of about 81,000 Medicare beneficiaries 65 and older living alone found that people who later developed dementia were more likely to miss payments on credit accounts beginning about six years before diagnosis, and more likely to have subprime credit scores starting about two and a half years before. The differences were small, so one late bill proves nothing. Look for patterns.

**Scam losses are growing.** The FBI's 2025 figures show adults 60 and older reported $7.7 billion in losses, about a 60% increase from 2024. In August 2025 the FTC reported that, from 2020 to 2024, reports of losses of $10,000 or more by adults 60 and older to impersonators more than quadrupled. Payment method is a tell. The FTC has reported gift cards, bank transfers, and Bitcoin ATMs among the ways impersonation scammers get paid.

**Suspicion isn't always dementia.** Paranoia in older adults can come from medication side effects, hearing and vision problems, delirium, and dementia, and many causes are treatable. If it comes on suddenly, call for an urgent appointment that includes evaluation for a new illness or medication side effect.

**Medication errors are easy to miss.** Duplicates are easy to create when several doctors and pharmacies are involved, because the same drug can go by both a brand name and a generic name.

## 3. What to do now

**Use the ladder, starting at the bottom.** State laws generally allow guardianship only as a last resort and require consideration of less restrictive alternatives, and your approach should follow the same logic.

1. **Observe and log** (details below).
2. **Talk and offer.** Use Chapter 5's scripts.
3. **Add safeguards that leave decisions with your parent.** Examples are a bank trusted contact, one pharmacy, and a "call me first" rule.
4. **Make support formal while capacity remains.** Supported decision-making lets people keep making their own decisions with support from trusted individuals they choose. Powers of attorney (Chapter 3) work the same way.
5. **Get a professional assessment** for a specific decision.
6. **Go to court** only when everything else has failed, and only with an elder law attorney.

**Topic playbooks**

| Concern | Signs | Gentle first step | Stronger step |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Money and scams** | Secret phone calls, urgent "government" demands, gift cards, wire or crypto requests, unopened bills, unusual purchases | "Call me before sending money to anyone who contacted you first." Add a trusted contact. | Bank fraud department, FTC report at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, or the National Elder Fraud Hotline (1-833-372-8311). The FTC says it will never demand money, make threats, or tell you to transfer money. |
| **Driving** | Unexplained dents and scrapes, trouble following signals, needing help with familiar routes | Ride along, then use Chapter 5's scripts | A driver rehabilitation specialist can test driving on and off the road and help decide when to stop. |
| **Medications** | Duplicates, missed doses, confusion about pills | Brown-bag review (Chapter 3) | One pharmacy and synchronized refills; blister packaging |
| **Suspicion** | New accusations, fear of intruders | Rule out medical causes first. Don't argue. | Doctor visit within days, sooner if sudden |

For any sudden, new suspicion, treat it as a Chapter 2 yellow. If there's violence, weapons, or a threat to anyone's safety, call 911.

**Document without building a case.** Your log (Chapter 2) exists to help professionals see patterns, not to prove your parent is incompetent.

- Record facts, dates, and what you saw, not labels like "paranoid."
- Include what's going *well*.
- Tell your parent: "I'm keeping a list of things to ask the doctor. Want to add anything?"
- Don't record conversations secretly. Federal law requires only one party's consent, but about 11 states generally require everyone's consent, so check your state's law. Secret recording also costs trust.
- Don't open mail, log into accounts, or move money without permission or legal authority.
- Share the log with doctors and your care team, not as ammunition in sibling disputes.

> **One family's story**
>
> Ruth wires $4,000 to a caller claiming to be her grandson in jail. She's embarrassed, and she insists she can still manage her own money. Marcus decides she's right: one bad decision doesn't mean she can't make decisions. Instead of taking over her accounts, he asks her to name him as a trusted contact at her bank and to set an alert for any withdrawal over $500. She keeps control, and when the next scam call comes, the bank catches it.

## 4. Exactly what to say

**Money:** "I'm not asking to see your accounts. I'd like us to have a rule: before you send money or gift cards to anyone who contacted you first, you call me. I won't say no. I just want a second set of ears."

**Bills:** "Would you let me be your backup? You'd keep paying them. The company would send me a copy, so nothing slips if you're ever sick."

**Medications:** "Could we ask the pharmacy to pack everything in a calendar card? Then you don't have to sort pills every week."

**Suspicion about a missing item:** "That sounds upsetting. Let's look together." *(Find it. Don't argue.)*

**Suspicion about someone entering the house:** "That sounds frightening. I want you to feel safe. Could Dr. Lee check whether a medicine or an infection could be making nights harder?"

**When they say "I'm still independent":** "You are, and I want to keep it that way. These two safeguards are so you can keep deciding."

**To a sibling who sees your log as a case:** "I'm keeping a log so Dr. Lee has facts. It isn't to prove anything about Mom."

## 5. Tool: The Independence and Risk Decision Matrix

**Step 1: Rate the risk.**

- **Low:** inconvenient and reversible (a messy kitchen, one late bill)
- **Moderate:** could cause harm over weeks or months but can be fixed (occasional missed pills, a near-miss while driving)
- **High:** serious or irreversible harm soon (payments to scammers, wrong or duplicated medicines, driving incidents)

**Step 2: Note your parent's response.** Do they accept help, feel ambivalent, or refuse (or lack insight)?

**Step 3: Find the zone.**

|  | **Accepts** | **Ambivalent** | **Refuses / lacks insight** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Low** | 🟢 *Respect.* Offer help. | 🟢 *Respect.* Mention once, then log. | 🟢 *Respect.* Log and revisit in 30 days. |
| **Moderate** | 🟡 *Partner.* Add a safeguard this month. | 🟡 *Partner.* Offer a choice of two safeguards. Involve the doctor. | 🟡 *Watch and escalate.* Log, try LEAP, get the doctor involved. |
| **High** | 🔴 *Act together this week.* | 🔴 *Protect.* Use the least restrictive safeguard now and consult a professional. | 🔴 *Protect now.* Doctor, professional assessment, and (as relevant) your bank, APS, the DMV, or an attorney. |

**Step 5: Before I step in, ask:**

- [ ] Have I ruled out a medical cause?
- [ ] Is this risky to them or someone else?
- [ ] Can it be undone?
- [ ] Have I tried the least restrictive step?
- [ ] Does my parent know what worries me?
- [ ] Is this a safety issue or only a difference in values?
- [ ] Has a doctor been involved?

## 6. When professional help is appropriate

- **The doctor:** start here to rule out medical causes. If cognitive impairment is suspected, Medicare covers a separate visit for a more detailed cognitive assessment and care plan. Medicare's description of that visit includes an evaluation of safety, including driving, and an assessment of the caregiver's needs and role.
- **A capacity evaluation:** a geriatrician, psychiatrist, or psychologist can assess capacity for a *specific* decision, such as managing money or signing a document.
- **Driving:** a Certified Driving Rehabilitation Specialist holds a credential from the Association for Driver Rehabilitation Specialists and is often an occupational therapist. The evaluation includes clinical and behind-the-wheel components, and may recommend adaptive equipment, retraining, or stopping.
- **Finances:** the bank's fraud department; Adult Protective Services if you suspect exploitation; and an elder law attorney for powers of attorney or, as a last resort, guardianship.
- **The pharmacist:** for a medication review.
- **You:** if you're lying awake over this, a counselor or caregiver group can help.

Start with the log. Every other rung on the ladder builds on it.
