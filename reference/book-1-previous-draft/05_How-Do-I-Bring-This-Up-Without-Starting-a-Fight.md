# Chapter 5: How Do I Bring This Up Without Starting a Fight?

*Days 15–21 of your 30-day plan*

## 1. The frustration

*"Every conversation ends with 'I'm fine,' 'It's none of your business,' or both."*

You rehearsed in the car. "Mom, I've been thinking..." Before you finish, she says "I'm fine," and the kitchen gets colder. You push. She says, "It's none of your business." Then you hear yourself say, "Well, somebody has to." Ten minutes later you're both angry, nothing has changed, and you've become the villain in her version of the story.

Most of the time, the trouble is the timing, the order you say things in, and a few phrases that land as attacks. Those are fixable.

## 2. What may be happening

**What you say is not what they hear.** You say "I'm worried." They hear "You're losing control." In AARP's 2024 Home and Community Preferences Survey, 75% of adults 50 and older said they wish to remain in their current homes, so every offer of help can sound like the first step toward leaving. Driving carries the same weight.

**Pushing backfires.** Motivational interviewing calls the urge to fix someone the "righting reflex." When you argue for change, an ambivalent person tends to take up and defend the opposite side. The alternative is to ask permission before giving advice.

**Sometimes it isn't stubbornness.** In dementia, people may lack awareness of their illness. When short-term memory fails, a person may not remember that they can't remember. The refusal is a symptom, not intentional denial. Facts won't win that argument. A method called LEAP (Listen, Empathize, Agree, Partner) is a non-confrontational approach designed to build trust.

**Tone matters more than you'd think.** Baby-talk-style "elderspeak" can backfire. In a small 2021 hospital study published in Innovation in Aging, a 15-percentage-point drop in staff elderspeak was linked to 62% lower odds that a person with dementia would reject care. The study involved just 16 patients and 53 staff members at one hospital, so treat it as a signal, not proof.

**You may not be the right messenger.** Sometimes the best move is to hand the message to someone your parent trusts, such as their doctor.

## 3. What to do now

**1. Start early, small, and private.** Pick a calm time, not a holiday dinner and not when either of you is tired. Take one topic. The Alzheimer's Association advises having the first conversation as soon as possible, and notes that the first attempt may not succeed, so write down what happened and what to change. Waiting is common. In a 2021 survey for the Alzheimer's Association and the Ad Council, only 44% of Americans said they would talk to a loved one right away about seeing a doctor after noticing cognitive decline.

**2. Use a four-part formula:** *your intention, one specific observation, a permission question, a small choice.* ("I want you to stay in this house. I noticed the stairs light is out. Can I fix that this week, or would you rather call the electrician?")

**3. Use the LEAP loop.** Listen without interrupting. Empathize ("That would frustrate me too"). Agree on a goal ("We both want you home"). Then partner on a step.

**4. Borrow authority.** Ask the doctor to raise the issue, or suggest an assessment instead of a verdict.

**5. When they refuse, don't escalate.** Say you'll revisit it, set a date, and log what happened. Refusal on a green topic can wait. A red flag from Chapter 2 can't. If safety is at stake, you may need to act through a doctor, Adult Protective Services, or the DMV even without agreement.

**6. Topic by topic:**

- **Driving.** Start before it's a problem, and don't open with scary risk statistics. Try a starter like "Driving isn't what it used to be." Line up rides first. A 2016 pooled analysis of five studies found that people who stopped driving had nearly twice the odds of depressive symptoms, though the studies couldn't show that stopping caused the depression.
- **Memory.** Frame it as a checkup: "Let's see if the doctor can help us figure out what's going on," and offer to go along. The Alzheimer's Association's free 24/7 Helpline is 800-272-3900.
- **Money.** Ask about safeguards, not amounts. Chapter 3's trusted-contact idea is a good first ask.
- **Home safety.** Tie every suggestion to staying home.

**7. Talk with doctors without speaking over your parent.** Plan together beforehand. Decide what issues to raise, who will raise them, and whether either of you wants time alone with the provider. Write the doctor a note in advance, let your parent and the doctor lead, and book a separate call or visit if you have more to say. Know that some patients won't speak honestly about sensitive subjects in front of a family member, and that some doctors will check partway through whether the patient is comfortable with you in the room. From Chapter 3, nothing stops you from telling a provider what you've noticed, even if they can't share anything back.

**8. Choose language that preserves dignity.**

| Instead of | Try |
| --- | --- |
| "You need to..." / "You can't..." | "Would you be open to...?" / "I'm worried about..." |
| "You always..." / "You never..." | One specific, recent example |
| "I'm taking over." | "I'd like to help with X. You stay in charge of Y." |
| "Remember when I told you?" | Just say it again, calmly. Don't try to jog the memory. |
| "That's not true!" / "Don't be silly." | "That sounds upsetting. Let's look together." |
| "Good job, sweetie!" | Adult tone. Talking down can feel patronizing and infantilizing. |

With accusations, ask yourself whether correcting is worth it. If your parent keeps accusing someone of taking a wallet, keep it in an easy-to-remember spot so you can show it's safe.

> **One family's story**
>
> At Thanksgiving, in front of everyone, Marcus tells Ruth she shouldn't be driving anymore. She leaves the table and doesn't speak to him for two days. In January, he tries again, privately and on the phone. He asks permission to bring it up, mentions the new dent in her car, and suggests a driving evaluation so she can prove him wrong. She agrees, because the decision is still hers.

## 4. Exactly what to say

Use this skeleton for any conversation:

**"I \[care about / want\] \_\_\_. I've noticed \_\_\_. Would you be open to \_\_\_? Or would you rather \_\_\_?"**

*Worked example (money):* "I want you to keep control of your money. I noticed a few bills went unopened. Would you be open to adding me as a trusted contact at the bank, so they call me if something looks like a scam? I wouldn't see your accounts. Or would you rather pick someone else?"

*Worked example (driving):* "I want you to keep your independence. I got nervous on Tuesday at the turn by the church. Would you be open to a driving evaluation, or would you rather start with Dr. Lee's opinion?"

## 5. Tool: Twenty-Five Difficult-Conversation Scripts

**Opening**

**1. First conversation:** "Mom, I'm not here to take anything over. I've noticed a few things, and I'd rather ask you than guess. Is now a good time?"

**2. Bad timing:** "It sounds like this isn't a good moment. Can we try Sunday after lunch?"

**3. After a blowup:** "I handled that badly. I care about you and I got pushy. Can we start over?"

**When they refuse**

**4. "I'm fine."** "I believe you want to be fine. I'd just feel better if we checked. What would be the easiest first step?"

**5. "It's none of your business."** "You're right that it's your life. I love you, so I worry. Can you tell me what part feels intrusive?"

**6. "I don't need help."** "I'm not saying you do. Could I help with one small thing, just so I stop worrying?"

**7. "Stop treating me like a child."** "You're right, and I'm sorry. How would you want me to bring things up?"

**Driving**

**8.** "Driving isn't what it used to be. Do you ever feel it's harder than it was?"

**9.** "Would you mind if I rode along Thursday? I'd like to see what you're seeing."

**10.** "I'd feel better with a professional evaluation. Would you do it for me? Or should we start with the doctor?"

**Memory**

**11.** "I've noticed we've both had some trouble remembering things lately. Let's see if the doctor can help us figure out what's going on."

**12.** "Would you let me come along? Two sets of ears are better than one."

**13. If they deny it:** "I hear you, and you may be right. A checkup will tell us either way."

**Money**

**14.** "I'm not asking how much you have. I'd just like to know where the papers are, in case something happens."

**15.** "There's a safeguard called a trusted contact. I'd have no access. Would you consider it?"

**16. If a scam is suspected:** "Before you send anything, would you call me first? It can be our rule for any request for money."

**Home safety**

**17.** "I want you to stay in this house. What would make it feel safer to you?"

**18.** "A therapist can check the home for free or at low cost. They look for trip hazards, not at you. Would you try it?"

**Anger, accusation, repetition, suspicion**

**19. Anger:** "I can see how upset you are. I'm going to step out for ten minutes and come back."

**20. "You're stealing from me":** "That sounds frightening. Let's look for it together." *(Don't argue.)*

**21. Repetition:** Answer the question again, in the same calm tone as the first time. Add a written note or calendar entry if it helps.

**22. Suspicion of you or a sibling:** "You have every right to ask questions. Shall we call the bank together and check?"

**Doctors**

**23. Before the visit:** "Is there anything you want me to bring up? Anything you'd rather say yourself?"

**24. In the room:** "Mom, you tell the doctor how you've been feeling, and then I'll add what I've noticed. Is that okay?"

**25. Privately, afterward:** "I want to share a concern without upsetting my mother. Could I send a note, or schedule a call?"

## 6. When professional help is appropriate

- **The doctor:** ask them to raise memory, driving, or medication concerns. Many parents will take it more seriously from a doctor than from a child.
- **Alzheimer's Association Helpline:** call the free 24/7 line, 800-272-3900, for scripts tailored to your situation.
- **Driving:** a doctor or occupational therapist can assess. If your parent refuses and is a danger, you can contact your local DMV, and some states accept anonymous reports while others don't.
- **Family conflict:** a geriatric care manager, social worker, or mediator can serve as the neutral voice.
- **Safety emergencies:** use Chapter 2. Suicidal talk means 988 or 911. Suspected abuse or exploitation means Adult Protective Services.
- **You:** if conversations leave you shaking or resentful, a counselor or caregiver group can help.

Pick one script and one topic this week. If it goes badly, write down what happened and try again.
