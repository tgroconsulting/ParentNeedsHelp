# Chapter 4: I Have a List—What Do I Actually Do With It?

*Days 11–14 of your 30-day plan*

## 1. The frustration

*"I understand the problems. I still don't know what action to take Monday morning."*

It's Monday, 7:15 a.m. Your coffee is going cold next to the assessment from Chapter 3. You've rated seven areas. Your three biggest risks are circled. You have twenty minutes before your first meeting, and you stare at a list that says things like *fix medications, make the house safer, get legal documents.*

Each item is a project, not a task. So you do what most people do, which is nothing, or something random like a Saturday spent rearranging your mother's pantry while the unpaid bills sit in the drawer.

What you need is a list of actions small enough to start today.

## 2. What may be happening

Lists freeze people when the items are the wrong size. "Fix Dad's medications" has no first step. "Photograph every bottle label on Sunday" does. A concern is a worry. An action is something you could do with your hands or your phone in under thirty minutes.

Specific plans work better than good intentions. A 2006 analysis by Gollwitzer and Sheeran, covering 94 independent tests, found that plans spelling out the when, where, and how of acting in advance had a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment. That's why every action in this chapter gets an owner, a date, and a trigger.

Two other forces work against you. The first is that medical appointments, where many of your actions run, leak information. A 2003 review in the Journal of the Royal Society of Medicine found that patients forget 40 to 80% of the medical information they are given almost immediately, and that almost half of what they do remember is incorrect. The second is that referrals leak too. In a study of one large health system covering July 2015 to June 2016, only about 35% of referral scheduling attempts ended in a documented completed appointment. That was a single system, but the point stands: if nobody tracks the referral, it can quietly disappear. Often that somebody is you.

Finally, don't try to repair everything at once. Your parent can absorb only so much change, and you can only do so much before you burn out. Doing things in order is how they actually get finished.

## 3. What to do now

**Step 1: Convert each concern into a next action.** A good next action is physical, visible, and under thirty minutes.

| Concern | Next action |
| --- | --- |
| "Dad's medications are a mess." | Photograph every bottle label Sunday. Ask the pharmacy for a printed list. |
| "Mom may not be safe in the shower." | Ask her doctor's office for an occupational therapy home-safety referral. |
| "Bills are piling up." | Sit with Mom for 30 minutes and open one week's mail together. |
| "There are no legal documents." | Call two elder law offices and ask about fees and the first opening. |
| "Dad's driving worries me." | Ride along Thursday and write down three specific observations. |

**Step 2: Give every action an owner, a date, and a trigger.** The owner is one name, not "we." Add a trigger: *"When I park at Mom's on Sunday, I'll photograph the bottles."* Remember Chapter 1, too. If the action involves your parent's life, your parent's role is to agree or help, not just to be informed.

**Step 3: Choose the first three.** Start from your top three risks, then filter. Can it be done in two weeks? Will your parent agree or at least not object? Can it be undone? Pick one action that's easy, because an early win builds your parent's trust. Everything else goes on a **parking lot** list with a review date. Keep no more than three actions active at once.

**Step 4: Prepare for appointments.** National Institute on Aging guidance on talking with your doctor is consistent. Make a prioritized list and bring up the biggest concerns first. Don't save what's on your mind for the end of the visit. Bring a list of every medication and supplement, or put the bottles in a bag. Bring a family member or friend who can take notes, and tell them in advance what you want from the visit. Also bring eyeglasses and hearing aids. Send your top three concerns through the patient portal beforehand if you can, and ask whether you should book a longer visit.

In the room, use teach-back. Repeat back what you think you heard. "Do you understand?" doesn't count, because real teach-back asks people to explain in their own words, not answer yes or no. If time runs out, ask to follow up by phone or email, book another visit, or speak with a nurse.

**Step 5: Track every referral until the loop closes.** A referral isn't done when it's ordered. It's done when the specialist has seen your parent and the report has reached the primary doctor. First, find out whether the plan requires one at all. Original Medicare doesn't require a referral to see a specialist, but Medicare Advantage plans may require a referral and prior authorization. Rules shift. For example, UnitedHealthcare announced in September 2025 that, starting January 1, 2026, most of its Medicare Advantage HMO and HMO-POS members would need a primary care referral for certain specialist services. Then, within three business days, call the specialist to confirm receipt, book the date, and log it.

**Step 6: Hold a 20-minute weekly review.** Every Sunday, check what's done, what's stuck, and whether anything on the parking lot can move up. One action finished means one parking-lot item can enter. If something becomes yellow or red, apply Chapter 2.

> **One family's story**
>
> Dana's list says "fix Dad's medications," and for three weeks nothing happens. When she shrinks it to "photograph every bottle on Sunday," she gets it done in fifteen minutes and finds that Frank uses three pharmacies and has two prescriptions for nearly the same blood pressure drug. After one pharmacist review, he's down to a single pharmacy and four fewer pills a day.

## 4. Exactly what to say

**To your parent, proposing a start:** "I don't want to change everything. There are three things on my list: the medications, the bathroom, and the bills. Which one would feel least intrusive to start with?"

**To a sibling, assigning an owner:** "Can you own the insurance piece? Call Mom's plan and find out whether she needs referrals or prior authorization. Can you finish by Friday and text me what you learn?"

**To the doctor's office, before the visit:** "I'm bringing my father Thursday. We have three concerns: \[one, two, three\]. Could you let the doctor know ahead of time, and tell me if we should book a longer visit?"

**In the exam room, before leaving:** "Let me make sure I have this right: \[repeat the plan\]. What should we watch for, and when should we call?"

**To a referral desk:** "I'm calling to confirm you received the referral for \[name, birthdate\] from Dr. \[X\] on \[date\]. What's the first available appointment, and will you send your notes back to Dr. \[X\]?"

**To anyone pressing you to do more:** "That's on the parking lot until \[date\]. We're finishing three things first."

## 6. When professional help is appropriate

- **Insurance and referral questions:** call your State Health Insurance Assistance Program. It provides free, objective counseling to Medicare-eligible people, their families, and caregivers, at 1-877-839-2675.
- **The doctor's office:** ask whether the practice has a care coordinator or nurse navigator who can track referrals.
- **A geriatric care manager:** consider one when you have more moving parts than you can track, such as several specialists, a recent hospitalization, or family conflict.
- **A hospital discharge planner or social worker:** if your parent has just been discharged, use this person before you go home.
- **A caregiver support group or counselor:** if you're missing your own appointments or sleep, tell someone. Your capacity is part of the plan.

Get three actions moving by Day 14. Everything else can wait on the parking lot.
