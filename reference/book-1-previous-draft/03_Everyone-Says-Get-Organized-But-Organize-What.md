# Chapter 3: Everyone Says "Get Organized," But Organize What?

*Days 6–10 of your 30-day plan*

## 1. The frustration

*"The advice sounds like common sense, but nobody tells me exactly what information I need."*

A friend says, "You just need to get organized." A website says, "Gather important documents." So you buy a three-ring binder, write MOM on the spine, and sit down with it. Then you stall. Which documents? From where? Does "important" include the electric bill? The binder stays empty, and now you feel behind on something you can't even define.

The advice is fine as far as it goes. It just stops too soon. What follows covers seven specific areas, what to find in each, and how to find it without turning every visit into a deposition.

## 2. What may be happening

Generic advice fails in three ways. It doesn't say what to collect, it treats every gap as equally urgent, and it assumes you can simply ask. Your parent may not know where things are, may be embarrassed that they don't, or may hear "Where's your will?" as "I'm planning your funeral."

You're building two things:

**A picture.** That means facts (names, numbers, documents) plus observations of how your parent actually functions. Professionals need both. "Dad's medications" is vague. "Dad takes eleven pills a day from three pharmacies" is something a doctor can act on.

**A ranking.** You won't fix seven areas in a month, so you need to know which three gaps could do the most harm soonest.

One area runs on a clock the others don't. Legal documents must be signed while your parent can still understand them. A dementia diagnosis doesn't automatically bar someone from signing, because what matters is capacity at the time, but if someone becomes incapacitated without a power of attorney, a court may have to appoint a guardian. That's why legal paperwork gets early attention even when everything else looks calm.

## 3. What to do now

### The seven areas

**1. Health.** Collect diagnoses, doctors' names and numbers, and insurance cards (Medicare, any supplement or Advantage plan, drug plan). Get written permission to talk to the doctors. Under the HIPAA privacy rule, providers aren't required to share information with family, though they may if you're involved in care and your parent doesn't object (HHS). Even if a provider can't tell you anything, nothing stops you from telling them what you've seen.

**2. Medications.** List every prescription, over-the-counter product, and supplement. In a 2020 University of Michigan National Poll on Healthy Aging of adults 50 to 80, about two in ten took five or more prescription medicines, and only 29% of those people had ever had a comprehensive medication review.

**3. Daily functioning.** Track two lists. The basic activities are feeding, continence, transferring, toileting, dressing, and bathing. The second list covers meals, shopping, medications, money, phone use, and transportation. Pay attention to the second list, because these more complex tasks typically decline before basic self-care does. Missed bills and pills often show up before bathing trouble.

**4. Home safety.** Walk through as a guest. CDC says falls are often caused by hazards that are easy to overlook but easy to fix. Look for loose rugs, clutter, stairs without handrails on both sides, a dark path from bed to bathroom, and no phone within reach at floor level.

**5. Transportation.** Is your parent still driving? Look for new dents, tickets, or getting lost. The CDC reports that about 9,100 older adults were killed in crashes in 2022, roughly 25 a day. The Insurance Institute for Highway Safety says the higher death rates for drivers 70 and older are mainly due to greater vulnerability to injury, not to being in more crashes. So this is about protecting your parent, not blaming them. Also ask who gets them to appointments if they stop driving.

**6. Finances.** Find income sources, regular bills, accounts, and where statements are kept. Watch for unopened mail or unusual withdrawals. In 2025, people 60 and older reported about $7.7 billion in fraud losses to the FBI, roughly 60% more than in 2024 (FBI figures as reported by AARP in April 2026). A "trusted contact" at the bank is one safeguard. A trusted contact generally can't make trades or decisions on the account. They're the person the institution can call if it sees signs of exploitation, and details vary by institution.

**7. Legal documents.** You're looking for a health care power of attorney, a financial power of attorney, a living will, a will, and the location of the originals. These are separate documents, because a financial power of attorney differs from a health care power of attorney, which covers only medical decisions. Gaps are common. A 2017 Health Affairs review of 150 studies conducted from 2011 to 2016 found that 36.7% of U.S. adults had completed an advance directive, and even among those 65 and older, the figure was 45.6%.

### Finding missing information without interrogating

- **Ask "where," not "what."** "Where do you keep your insurance cards?" is easier than "What insurance do you have?"
- **Borrow a reason.** "I'm updating my own paperwork and realized I don't have yours."
- **Look, don't inspect.** Notice the mail pile, the pill bottles, the fridge, and the car, then write it down in the car afterward.
- **Go along.** Ask to join one appointment, and ask the pharmacy for a printed medication list.
- **One question per visit.** Small asks feel like help. A list of twenty feels like an audit.
- **Use other witnesses.** Siblings, neighbors, and longtime friends often know things your parent hasn't told you.

### Naming your three biggest risks

Rate each area 🟢 fine for now, 🟡 needs a plan this month, or 🔴 likely harm within weeks. Then pick no more than three, using these questions. Could this cause serious harm soon? Is it hard to undo? Is there a deadline, like the capacity window? Those three drive your 30-day plan. Everything else waits.

### Turning observations into notes for professionals

Write what happened, when, and how often, without a diagnosis. Compare "Mom is forgetful" with "Since March, Mom has missed three appointments, paid the water bill twice, and called twice in one evening to ask the same question." The second version gives a doctor something to work with. Keep it to one page and attach the medication list.

> **One family's story**
>
> Carmen Reyes, 84, lives near her daughter Elena, who does most of the hands-on help. Her other daughter, Sofia, lives across the country.
>
> Helping Carmen look for her insurance card, Elena comes across her mother's power of attorney. It was signed in 1998 and names Elena's father, who died six years ago. Nobody else is named as a backup. The sisters book an elder law attorney that month, while Carmen can still understand and sign new documents.

## 4. Exactly what to say

**About papers:** "Mom, I'm getting my own paperwork in order, and it made me realize I don't know where yours is. You don't have to show me anything. Could you just tell me where you keep it, so I'd know where to look if something happened?"

**About the bank:** "I'm not asking about your money. There's an option called a trusted contact. I'd have no access to your accounts. The bank would only call me if something looked like a scam. Would you be okay with that?"

**About medications:** "Would you do something with me? We put every bottle and supplement in a bag and let the pharmacist look it over. Your plan may even cover it."

**About doctors:** "Would you sign a form so Dr. Lee can talk to me and I can see your portal? You'd still make every decision. I'd just stop having to guess."

**When they say "That's private":** "That's fair, and I won't push. Can we agree on one person who knows where everything is if you're ever in the hospital? It can be me, your sister, or your attorney."

**To a professional:** "I've put together a one-page summary of what I've noticed. I'm not asking you to diagnose anything. I'd like to know what you think we should check first."

## 6. When professional help is appropriate

*Laws vary by state, and this is general information, not legal advice.*

- **Primary care doctor:** bring your one-page summary. Ask for the Medicare annual wellness visit. It costs nothing once every 12 months if the provider accepts Medicare's approved amount, and it includes a cognitive assessment. If impairment is suspected, Medicare covers a separate, more thorough cognitive visit.
- **Pharmacist:** ask about a comprehensive medication review, or do the "brown bag" version with every bottle.
- **Elder law attorney:** call soon if documents are missing, especially if memory is a concern. Capacity is time-sensitive.
- **Occupational therapist or geriatric care manager:** for a professional home-safety assessment or when the picture feels too tangled to sort alone.
- **Bank or financial advisor:** to add a trusted contact. For a suspected scam, the National Elder Fraud Hotline is 1-833-372-8311.
- **Adult Protective Services:** if you suspect exploitation or neglect. As in Chapter 2, you don't need proof.

Take the whole week for this. Three risks and a one-page summary are enough to move on.
