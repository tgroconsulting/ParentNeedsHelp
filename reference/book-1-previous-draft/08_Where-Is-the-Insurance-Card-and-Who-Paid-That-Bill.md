# Chapter 8: Where Is the Insurance Card—and Who Paid That Bill?

*Days 29–30 of your 30-day plan*

## 1. The frustration

It's 6:40 p.m. and the ER nurse asks for the insurance card, the medication list, and the name of your mother's cardiologist. You're standing in her kitchen, opening drawers. The card isn't in her wallet. The medication list is a photo on your sister's phone. The cardiologist's name is on a business card that is "in the blue folder," which is not in the blue folder.

Neither of you did anything wrong. Information that worked fine while one person carried it in their head has to be findable by someone else, under pressure. About 63 million Americans, nearly one in four adults, provided care for a family member or friend in 2025, according to AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving's *Caregiving in the US 2025*. Many learn this lesson mid-crisis. You're going to learn it now.

## 2. What may be happening

Three things are usually going on at once.

- **The records are scattered by design.** Insurers mail paper, doctors use portals, banks use apps, and each has its own login. Your parent may know exactly where everything is. That knowledge just isn't written down.
- **Nobody has been named.** Without authorization paperwork, offices can't talk to you. Without a central place, you can't hand the job to anyone else.
- **The money is mixed.** The first time you put a pharmacy bill on your own card "just this once," you start a record-keeping problem. It affects your parent's taxes, your own budget, and, later, your siblings' trust.

One caution before you gather everything in one place. The information that rescues you in an ER is also what a scammer wants. Medicare cards no longer carry a Social Security number; they carry a randomly generated Medicare Beneficiary Identifier. It still shouldn't be photographed and texted around.

## 3. What to do now

**Day 29: build the core (about two hours).**

1. **Print the binder** and start with page 2, the Emergency Quick Sheet. Print two copies: one for the fridge, one for your bag.
2. **Fill pages 3 through 5 from the real objects**, not from memory. Use the pill bottles for medications, the cards for insurance (copy front and back), and the business cards or portal pages for doctors.
3. **Use page 6 to record where the legal documents are.** Don't copy their contents. Keep copies in the binder and put the originals in a fireproof safe or with the attorney. A safe deposit box can be a poor home for a power of attorney. An agent may need the original to get into the box, and banks can restrict access after a death. Rules vary by state, so ask an elder law attorney.

**Day 30: money, access, routines.**

4. **Start the expense log (page 8) today.** The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's guidance for agents under a power of attorney comes down to four duties: act only in your parent's interest, manage their money carefully, keep your money separate from theirs, and keep good records. Even without a power of attorney, the habit protects you. It also matters at tax time. If your parent is your dependent, you may be able to deduct, if you itemize, the part of the medical costs you paid that exceeds 7.5% of your adjusted gross income (IRS Publication 502).
5. **Fill the Accounts and Digital Access Index (page 10) as a map, not a vault.** Write where each login is kept and who can reach it, never the login itself. Put the actual passwords in a password manager with an emergency-access feature. Bitwarden (a paid Premium feature), LastPass, and Keeper offer a trusted contact who can request access after a waiting period you set. As of this writing, 1Password's support pages describe a printed Emergency Kit instead of an emergency-access feature. These features change, so check each company's current plan details.
6. **Put a 10-minute monthly appointment on your calendar** for page 12. Part of that appointment is reading the Medicare Summary Notice. Original Medicare mails one every three months when it was billed, or monthly if your parent signs up for electronic notices. Look for any service your parent didn't receive. You can report suspicious billing to 1-800-MEDICARE (1-800-633-4227) or the Senior Medicare Patrol (1-877-808-2468).

**Sharing without exposing.** Most people only need a slice of this information.

- Paramedics and the ER need page 2.
- A backup caregiver gets the whole binder.
- Everyone else gets last-4-digit references.
- Use official delegations instead of your parent's passwords: a signed HIPAA authorization at each doctor's office, patient-portal proxy access, and the bank's "trusted contact" or power-of-attorney form.
- Don't text photos of cards or email passwords.

> **One family's story**
>
> When Frank is taken to the ER after a dizzy spell, Dana stands at the registration desk unable to answer basic questions. She doesn't know his full medication list, his cardiologist's name, or where his Medicare card is. The next weekend she builds his emergency binder, and she keeps a one-page summary on his fridge and in her phone.

## 4. Exactly what to say

**To your parent, introducing the binder:** "Mom, I'm not trying to take anything over. I want one place with your doctors, your pills, and where the papers are, so that if something happens, nobody's guessing. You decide what goes in it and who gets to see it."

**To your parent, about money:** "When I pay something for you, I'll write it down and keep the receipt. We'll settle up on the first of the month so we never have to wonder."

**To a doctor's office or bank:** "I help my father with his care. What forms do you need on file so I can speak with you or see his statements? Can you add me as an authorized contact?"

**To your siblings:** "I've set up an emergency binder. Here's the one-page Quick Sheet for everyone. \[Name\] will hold a full backup copy. On the first of each month I'll send a short update from the log."

**To a caller asking for a Medicare number:** "Medicare doesn't call to ask for that. I'm hanging up and calling 1-800-MEDICARE."

## 6. When professional help is appropriate

- **An elder law attorney** when no power of attorney or health care directive exists, when your parent's capacity is changing (see Chapter 6), or for questions about trusts, property, or Medicaid planning.
- **A tax preparer** when you're paying a parent's medical costs and want to claim them correctly.
- **Your State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP)** for free, unbiased Medicare counseling, especially during fall enrollment.
- **Adult Protective Services, and the police if money is missing,** if you suspect someone is exploiting your parent financially. Use the Senior Medicare Patrol for suspected Medicare fraud.
