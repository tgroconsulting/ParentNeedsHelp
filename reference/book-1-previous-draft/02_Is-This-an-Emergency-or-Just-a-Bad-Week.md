# Chapter 2: Is This an Emergency or Just a Bad Week?

*Days 3–5 of your 30-day plan*

## 1. The frustration

*"Every problem feels urgent, and I can't tell what needs attention today versus next month."*

It's Sunday, 8:40 p.m. Your mom's cough sounds worse than it did on Friday. Your dad's blood pressure refill is two days late. Your brother has texted, "Should we take her in?" A neighbor mentioned your dad seemed unsteady at the mailbox. All of it arrives at the same volume.

So you do one of two things. You rush to the ER over a cold, or you tell yourself it's probably nothing and go to bed, and then you lie awake. Both are what happens when you have worries but no system for sorting them.

The three-color system below is meant to fill that gap.

## 2. What may be happening

Part of why everything feels urgent is that you don't have a baseline yet. You don't know what "normal" looks like for your parent's walking, thinking, eating, and sleeping, so every change looks equally alarming.

The other part is that older adults don't follow the textbook, which means a bad week can be an emergency in disguise. Heart trouble in older adults is a good example. A 2022 American Heart Association scientific statement notes that heart attacks in people 75 and older are more likely to happen without chest pain, and may show up as shortness of breath, fainting, or sudden confusion. Infection works the same way. Older adults may not run a high fever with a serious infection. A urinary tract infection can cause sudden confusion without the usual urinary symptoms. That matters because infection can progress to sepsis, and in 2008 U.S. hospital data, adults 65 and older were 13 times more likely than younger adults to be hospitalized with it.

Medications are another hidden risk. A CDC-led study of 2007–2009 data from 58 hospitals, published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 2011, estimated nearly 100,000 emergency hospitalizations a year for adverse drug events in adults 65 and older. Four kinds of medicine (warfarin, insulin, antiplatelet drugs, and diabetes pills) accounted for 67% of them, and about 66% involved unintentional overdoses.

The reverse is also true. Most bad weeks are just bad weeks. Your job is to sort quickly and consistently, so you act fast on the real emergencies without letting every worry run your life. You don't need to be a nurse to do that.

*A note: this chapter is a guide, not a diagnosis. When you're unsure, call 911. The dispatcher will help you decide.*

## 3. What to do now

Use three colors. For anything that worries you, ask: *Is this sudden? Could it cause serious harm in the next few hours? Is it different from my parent's normal?*

### 🔴 RED: Call 911 now

- **Stroke signs (BE FAST):** sudden trouble with balance or vision, facial drooping, arm weakness, or slurred speech. Call 911 even if the symptoms go away. Note the time they started. Don't drive your parent yourself, because paramedics can alert the hospital so the stroke team is ready.
- **Chest pressure, unexplained trouble breathing, or sudden confusion with dizziness**
- **Suspected sepsis:** an infection plus confusion, fast breathing, extreme fatigue, clammy skin, or severe pain.
- **A fall with a head strike while on blood thinners.** Seek emergency care even if symptoms seem mild, and call 911 for any weakness, numbness, slurred speech, or new confusion.
- **A fall where they can't get up,** or have severe pain or an obvious deformity
- **Talk of suicide with a plan or means.** Call 911 if someone may be in imminent danger.

### 🟡 YELLOW: Call a clinician today

- **A sudden change in thinking or alertness** without red-flag signs. This is the delirium pattern from Chapter 1.
- **Any fall,** even without injury. A head bump without blood thinners still deserves a same-day call, and the doctor may want to see your parent in person.
- **A medication mix-up:** double doses, skipped insulin or blood thinners, wrong pills. Call the pharmacist or Poison Help. The free, confidential line, 1-800-222-1222, is open around the clock and staffed by nurses, pharmacists, and doctors. HRSA says people usually get the help they need over the phone.
- **Not eating or drinking for a day or more,** new urinary symptoms, or a wound that's red, warm, or worsening
- **Possible exploitation or mistreatment.** You don't need proof to report to Adult Protective Services. For a suspected scam, the National Elder Fraud Hotline is 1-833-372-8311.
- **Hopelessness, giving things away, or saying goodbye.** Dialing or texting 988 connects family and caregivers, not just the person in crisis, to counselors around the clock. In 2024, men 75 and older had the highest suicide rate of any age group, 40.1 deaths per 100,000, according to the National Center for Health Statistics.

### 🟢 GREEN: Goes into the 30-day plan

Piled-up mail, forgetfulness that has crept in over months, a cluttered house, hearing or vision decline, a single missed appointment, loneliness, sibling friction, no legal documents. Green doesn't mean ignore. It means *log it and schedule it.*

**The tiebreaker: when in doubt, go one color up.** Being wrong in that direction costs you an embarrassing phone call. Being wrong the other way can cost far more.

> **One family's story**
>
> Marcus, 46, lives 700 miles from his mother, Ruth, 78, who lives alone. He sees her twice a year and relies on phone calls and a helpful neighbor.
>
> On a Sunday, the neighbor calls: Ruth seems confused and keeps asking what day it is. Marcus assumes her memory is finally slipping and plans to raise it at her next checkup. Then he remembers that this came on suddenly, which makes it yellow, not green. He calls her doctor's after-hours line. It's a urinary tract infection, and within a week of antibiotics Ruth is herself again.

## 4. Exactly what to say

**To a 911 dispatcher:** "My father is 82. Since about 2:15 he's had slurred speech and his left arm is weak. He takes a blood thinner. We're at \[address\]."

**To the ER triage nurse:** "Mom was walking and talking normally yesterday. Today she's confused and sleepy. She had a urinary infection in the spring. I'm concerned about sepsis." Naming sepsis out loud may prompt staff to check for it.

**To the doctor's office (yellow):** "I'm calling about my mother, \[name, birthdate\]. Since yesterday she's been more confused than usual, which is a sudden change for her. She takes \[medications\]. I'd like her seen today. If that's not possible, what should we do?"

**To a parent who says "Don't make a fuss":** "I'm not overruling you. I'm calling because this is the kind of thing doctors want to hear about right away. If I'm wrong, we've lost ten minutes."

**To a parent refusing 911 during a red-flag moment:** "I know you don't want to go. I'm having the paramedics check you out. You can decide what happens after they're here."

**To your siblings, afterward:** "Update: \[what happened, when\]. \[Who we called, what they said\]. Next step is \[X\]. Logged in the shared file."

## 5. Tool: The 72-Hour Family Triage Checklist

**Hour 0–1: Scan.**

- [ ] Check every red flag above. Any yes means call 911.
- [ ] Write down the exact time symptoms started.

**Hours 1–24: Sort.**

- [ ] List every worry and mark it 🔴 🟡 🟢.
- [ ] Make every yellow call today.
- [ ] Tell one sibling what you decided.

**Hours 24–72: Build your safety net.**

- [ ] Save these in your phone and your parent's: 911, doctor, pharmacy, Poison Help (1-800-222-1222), 988, Eldercare Locator (1-800-677-1116).
- [ ] Make a one-page **Grab Sheet:** full name, birthdate, address, allergies, diagnoses, every medication and dose, doctors' numbers, insurance, emergency contact, and a line on their baseline (how they normally walk, talk, and think). Keep copies in your phone and on the fridge.
- [ ] Ask the pharmacy for a printed medication list.
- [ ] Identify who lives closest and who has a key.
- [ ] Start the log below.

## 6. When professional help is appropriate

- **Any red flag:** 911. Don't wait to see if it passes.
- **Yellow:** the doctor's office the same day. If there's no appointment, ask for the nurse line or an urgent care referral.
- **Medication confusion:** a pharmacist can review every prescription and supplement, and Poison Help answers urgent questions.
- **Several yellows in a month:** that's a pattern. Ask the doctor about a geriatric assessment, or consider a geriatric care manager.
- **Suspected abuse, neglect, or severe self-neglect:** contact Adult Protective Services in the state where your parent lives. The Eldercare Locator can help you find the right office.
- **You:** if you're not sleeping, a counselor or caregiver support group is part of the plan.

If you only do one thing this week, save the phone numbers and write the Grab Sheet.
