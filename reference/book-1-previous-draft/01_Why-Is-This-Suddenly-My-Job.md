# Chapter 1: Why Is This Suddenly My Job?

*Days 1–3 of your 30-day plan*

## 1. The frustration

*"I know something needs to change, but I don't know what I'm responsible for, or whether I'm overreacting."*

You're sitting in your car in the work parking lot, rereading a text from your sister: "Have you seen Dad's mail? It's all over the kitchen table." You have. You saw it three weeks ago, along with the dent in his bumper, the expired milk in the fridge, and the story he told you twice in one phone call. You told yourself he was tired.

Now you're wondering whether this is a real problem or whether you're a worrier turning normal aging into a crisis. Underneath that is a harder question: if it is real, whose job is it? Nobody called a meeting or handed you a folder. You noticed, and noticing is how most people become caregivers.

You're in large company. In its 2025 report, AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving estimated that 63 million Americans, nearly one in four adults, provide care for a family member or friend. About 59 million of them care for an adult, and the total is up about 20 million since 2015. Few of them applied for the role.

This chapter is the job description nobody gave you.

## 2. What may be happening

Aging rarely announces itself. It shows up as small things that each have a reasonable excuse, until the excuses stop adding up. One missed appointment is an incident. A missed appointment plus unopened mail plus a dented bumper is a pattern. Noticing a pattern isn't overreacting.

The numbers explain why waiting is risky. According to the CDC, one in four adults 65 and older reports falling each year, and falls are the leading cause of injury and of injury-related death in this age group. In a CDC survey of 2014 data, about 37% of those who fell reported an injury that needed medical treatment or limited their activity, and about half had not talked to a health care provider about the fall. Your parent may be falling more than you know.

One distinction matters right now: **speed**. Gradual change over months is a pattern to track. Sudden change is a medical question. Delirium is a sudden shift in attention, thinking, and alertness, and in older adults it can be the first sign of a medical problem. Infections, especially urinary tract infections and pneumonia, are a common cause, and so are medications. The VA notes that delirium comes on quickly, over days, while dementia comes on slowly, over months to years. Confusion that appears suddenly isn't "just getting older." Call the doctor the same day.

Two traps catch almost every new caregiver:

**Taking over.** You see a problem and fix it, whether or not anyone asked. It feels efficient, but it erodes your parent's dignity and quietly makes you responsible for everything.

**Staying out.** "It's not my place" feels respectful, but it usually means waiting for a crisis, when options are fewer and costs are higher.

The way through is knowing who controls what:

- **Your parent** controls their own life, including where they live, whom they see, which treatment they accept, and how they spend their money. Adults are presumed capable of making these choices. Forgetfulness, or a decision you disagree with, doesn't change that.
- **Doctors** make medical judgments: diagnoses, treatment recommendations, and whether a person can understand and weigh a particular decision.
- **Professionals** (attorneys, financial advisors, care managers, home care agencies) control their own areas of expertise.
- **Family** controls what each person chooses to contribute. No sibling controls another.
- **You** control your own role: what you offer, what you decline, and how you organize the effort.

Notice what's missing from that list: deciding for your parent.

## 3. What to do now

**Step 1: Name your role.** Say it plainly: *I coordinate help. I don't run a life.* A coordinator gathers information, connects people, and follows up. Your parent decides.

**Step 2: Write down what you've seen.** Spend ten minutes listing specifics with dates. "Dad seems off" is hard for a doctor to use. "March 3: missed his cardiology appointment; March 10: three unpaid bills; March 14: told me the same story twice" gets attention.

**Step 3: Sort the problem.** For each issue, ask: *whose decision is this?* If the answer is "my parent's," your job is to inform and support, not to override.

**Step 4: Set your limits before you're exhausted.** Caregivers who skip this step tend to pay for it. In AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving's 2025 report, nearly one in four caregivers provided 40 or more hours of care per week, about one in five reported fair or poor health, and nearly half reported at least one major financial impact, such as taking on debt.

**Step 5: Know what you're not going to get from Medicare.** Many families assume it will pay for ongoing help. Medicare doesn't pay for long-term care, meaning help with bathing, dressing, and using the bathroom, whether at home, in assisted living, or in a nursing home. Short-term rehab after a hospital stay is a different category. For scale, CareScout's 2025 Cost of Care Survey (released March 2026) reported national medians of $35 an hour for an in-home non-medical caregiver, $74,400 a year for assisted living, and $114,975 a year for a semi-private nursing home room. Your area may differ. Knowing this now will change a lot of what you decide later.

**Step 6: Tell your siblings.** Send a short, factual update now, even if they're distant.

> **One family's story**
>
> Dana, 52, lives 20 minutes from her widowed father, Frank, 81. Her brother lives in Denver. Because she's closest, she becomes the default caregiver without anyone deciding it.
>
> When Dana finds a stack of overdue bills on Frank's kitchen table, she quietly starts paying them online. Frank discovers it and is furious that she went into his accounts. After a few days, she apologizes and tries again: "I'd like to help with the bills. Do you want me to, or would you rather we set up autopay together?" He chooses autopay. Dana realizes her job is to coordinate, not to take over.

## 4. Exactly what to say

**To your parent, opening the conversation:** "Dad, I'm not here to take anything over. I've noticed a few things, and I'd rather ask you than guess. Can I tell you what I've seen, and you tell me if I've got it wrong?"

**When they say "I'm fine":** "I believe you want to be fine, and I'm not saying you're not. I'd just feel better if we checked. Would you rather start with a doctor visit, or with me going through the mail with you?"

*Offering a choice between two small steps keeps the decision in their hands.*

**To your siblings:** "I want everyone in the loop. Here's what I've seen: \[two or three dated specifics\]. I'm willing to coordinate, but I can't do everything. Can we get on a call this week and divide this up?"

**To anyone who assumes you'll handle it all:** "I can take on \[specific task\]. I can't take on \[specific task\]. Let's figure out who can."

## 6. When professional help is appropriate

**Call 911** for chest pain, trouble breathing, or signs of a stroke such as a drooping face, slurred speech, or sudden weakness.

**Call the doctor the same day** for sudden confusion, fever, a new fall, or medication mix-ups.

**Call a geriatric care manager** (usually a nurse or social worker) if the situation feels tangled and you need someone to assess needs and map options.

**Call an elder law attorney** if you're unsure about powers of attorney, health care directives, or protecting assets. Chapter 3 covers documents.

**Call the Eldercare Locator** at 1-800-677-1116 to find your parent's local Area Agency on Aging. It's a free public service of the Administration for Community Living.

**Seek counseling or a caregiver support group** if guilt, resentment, or exhaustion is building. Looking after yourself is part of the job.

You've named your role. Next comes your first hard judgment call: whether what you're seeing is an emergency or just a bad week.
