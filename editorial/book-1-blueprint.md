## Book positioning

**Target audience:** Adult children who have recently realized that one or both parents may need additional help, whether because of a sudden crisis or gradual changes.

**Central reader problem:** Something has changed, and the reader does not know where to begin, what matters most, or how much responsibility they should take on.

**Book promise:** By the end of this book, readers will understand how to organize the situation, identify immediate priorities, communicate with their parents and family, find appropriate resources, and create a manageable first-month plan.

**Important distinction:** This is not a medical or financial advice book, a dementia handbook, or a complete caregiving manual. It is a practical starting point.

## Chapter 1 — Something Has Changed
### Recognizing When an Aging Parent May Need Help

**Opening frustration:** "Things aren't quite right with Mom or Dad anymore. I can see it, but I don't know whether I'm overreacting."

**Purpose:** Help readers understand that caregiving often begins with uncertainty rather than a dramatic event. Give them a realistic way to recognize changes, observe concerns, and decide what deserves attention.

**What this chapter must accomplish:**
1. Establish how easily small changes can go unnoticed or be explained away.
2. Explore several starting points: a fall, hospitalization, missed obligations, household changes, social withdrawal, or a gradual increase in dependence.
3. Explain the difference between observing something concerning and deciding what caused it.
4. Help the reader document specific observations rather than relying on vague impressions.
5. Discuss the importance of involving the parent in conversations rather than jumping immediately to control.
6. Clearly distinguish routine concerns from situations that require prompt professional evaluation or immediate emergency assistance.
7. Introduce the idea that the first job is understanding the situation—not trying to solve everything.

**Practical deliverable:** A one-page "What I've Noticed" worksheet recording observed changes, dates, concerns, and unanswered questions.

**Chapter boundary:** Do not diagnose dementia, explain disease progression, or provide detailed medical assessments. Those belong with qualified professionals and, where appropriate, later books.

**Reader's final outcome:** "I may not know exactly what's happening, but I can explain what I've noticed and identify what needs attention."

## Chapter 2 — Is This an Emergency or Just a Bad Week?

**Author direction (2026-11-02):**
- Preserve the red-yellow-green system.
- Ensure examples represent both cognitive and physical health changes.
- Where supported by reliable sources, include or strengthen examples involving: breathing difficulty; sudden weakness; falls and head injuries; uncontrolled or rapidly worsening symptoms; medication mistakes or reactions; dehydration or inability to eat and drink; changes after surgery or hospital discharge; sudden confusion or delirium.
- Do not create exhaustive medical symptom lists.
- Do not imply that the reader can diagnose a condition. The reader's responsibility is to notice change and contact the appropriate professional.
- Add a line directing families with recent discharge instructions to use the hospital's or clinician's after-hours number when appropriate.
- Preserve the instruction to call 911 when red-flag symptoms are present.

## Chapter 3 — Everyone Says "Get Organized," But Organize What?

**Author direction (2026-11-02):**
- Broaden the Seven-Area Aging Parent Assessment.
- Health and functioning sections must capture: current diagnoses; date and reason for the most recent hospitalization; recent surgery or procedure; treating specialists; treatment or therapy schedule; medication changes; discharge instructions; activity or lifting restrictions; PT, OT, or home-health involvement; mobility changes; pain, fatigue, appetite and hydration concerns; durable medical equipment; transportation needs; the parent's normal physical and mental baseline.
- Add discharge papers, treatment schedules and rehabilitation instructions to the records readers may need to locate.
- Use the diagnosis-first family to demonstrate gathering information without taking over (author chose a new family: the Lindqvists).
- Do not turn the assessment into a medical chart. Keep it usable by an ordinary family member.

## Chapter 4 — What's Actually Going On?
### Separating Facts, Assumptions, and Family Opinions

**Opening frustration:** "My sister thinks Dad is fine. I think something is wrong. Dad says we're both making a big deal out of nothing."

**Purpose:** Help readers develop a clearer picture of the parent's circumstances before making major decisions.

**What this chapter must accomplish:**
1. Explain why different family members can have very different views of the same situation.
2. Distinguish what someone directly observed from what they heard, inferred, or assumed.
3. Introduce the main areas of daily life that may deserve attention: meals, transportation, household responsibilities, social connections, mobility, and appointments.
4. Show how to ask questions without turning conversations into interrogations.
5. Discuss how distance, infrequent visits, and incomplete information create blind spots.
6. Explain how to document concerns respectfully while protecting privacy.
7. Help the reader identify what remains unknown and who might appropriately help clarify it.

**Practical deliverable:** A "Current Situation Snapshot" with categories for observations, parent preferences, unknowns, and next steps.

**Chapter boundary:** This is not a formal clinical, cognitive, or home-safety assessment.

**Reader's final outcome:** "I have a clearer picture of the situation and know what information is still missing."

## Chapter 5 — Making Sense of the Appointments
### Being Helpful Without Becoming the Doctor

**Opening frustration:** "Mom went to three appointments this month, and none of us seems to know what happened at any of them."

**Purpose:** Teach readers how to help coordinate healthcare information and appointments without attempting to make medical decisions themselves.

**What this chapter must accomplish:**
1. Explain common communication challenges when several providers, appointments, and family members are involved.
2. Show readers how to prepare a concise list of questions before an appointment.
3. Explain how to record information and follow-up instructions accurately.
4. Discuss the parent's permission and appropriate authorization for sharing medical information.
5. Suggest practical ways to keep track of appointments, provider contact details, and unanswered questions.
6. Explain how to request clarification when instructions are confusing or incomplete.
7. Describe how to identify the appropriate healthcare professional to contact when follow-up questions arise.

**Practical deliverable:** A reusable appointment preparation and follow-up worksheet.

**Chapter boundary:** No treatment recommendations, medication changes, diagnoses, or individualized medical advice. Book 4 will provide the comprehensive healthcare information-management system.

**Reader's final outcome:** "I can help make appointments more productive and keep track of what needs to happen afterward."

## Chapter 6 — Is Home Still Working?
### Looking at Everyday Independence and Support

**Opening frustration:** "Every time I visit, I find something else that hasn't been taken care of."

**Purpose:** Help readers recognize when everyday tasks may be becoming more difficult for a parent and how to explore supportive changes without immediately assuming they must leave their home.

**What this chapter must accomplish:**
1. Explain why remaining at home can be deeply important to older adults.
2. Introduce common areas of concern: mobility, meals, transportation, household upkeep, accessibility, and social isolation.
3. Discuss how to notice patterns rather than overreact to an isolated incident.
4. Encourage conversations about what the parent finds difficult and what assistance they would welcome.
5. Present examples of everyday support, such as help with shopping, transportation, chores, or community services.
6. Explain when professional home-safety or functional assessments may be appropriate.
7. Help readers identify one or two realistic adjustments instead of immediately pursuing major changes.

**Practical deliverable:** A home and daily-life observation checklist, including support needs and parent preferences.

**Chapter boundary:** Do not declare a home safe or unsafe, determine whether the parent can live independently, or prescribe renovations. Book 3 will explore independence and safety more deeply.

**Reader's final outcome:** "I know which daily activities may need support and how to discuss possible changes."

## Chapter 7 — The Information Nobody Can Find
### Getting the Essentials Organized

**Opening frustration:** "Nobody knows the name of Dad's specialist, which pharmacy he uses, or who to call if something happens."

**Purpose:** Show readers how to build a simple, accessible information system that reduces confusion when help is needed.

**What this chapter must accomplish:**
1. Explain how missing information creates avoidable delays and confusion.
2. Identify the basic contact information families may need, including providers, pharmacies, emergency contacts, and relevant service organizations.
3. Show how to create a centralized list of appointments, follow-ups, and important questions.
4. Discuss how to organize documents without collecting more sensitive information than necessary.
5. Explain the importance of the parent's consent, secure storage, and appropriate access.
6. Introduce the idea of identifying important documents and where they are kept without interpreting their legal or financial meaning.
7. Demonstrate how to keep information updated as circumstances change.

**Practical deliverable:** An "Essential Information Organizer" containing contacts, document locations, current responsibilities, and outstanding tasks.

**Chapter boundary:** Do not recommend estate-planning documents, interpret legal authorization, or provide financial planning advice.

**Reader's final outcome:** "I have a practical system for keeping essential information organized and accessible to the right people."
