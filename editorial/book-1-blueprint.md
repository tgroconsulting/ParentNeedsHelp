## Book positioning

**Target audience:** Adult children who have recently realized that one or both parents may need additional help, whether because of a sudden crisis or gradual changes.

**Central reader problem:** Something has changed, and the reader does not know where to begin, what matters most, or how much responsibility they should take on.

**Book promise:** By the end of this book, readers will understand how to organize the situation, identify immediate priorities, communicate with their parents and family, find appropriate resources, and create a manageable first-month plan.

**Important distinction:** This is not a medical or financial advice book, a dementia handbook, or a complete caregiving manual. It is a practical starting point.

## Chapter 1 — Something Has Changed
### Recognizing When an Aging Parent May Need Help

**Opening frustration:** "Things aren't quite right with Mom or Dad anymore. I can see it, but I don't know whether I'm overreacting."

**Purpose:** Help readers understand that caregiving often begins with uncertainty rather than a dramatic event. Give them a realistic way to recognize changes, observe concerns, and decide what deserves attention.

**What this chapter must accomplish:**
1. Establish how easily small changes can go unnoticed or be explained away.
2. Explore several starting points: a fall, hospitalization, missed obligations, household changes, social withdrawal, or a gradual increase in dependence.
3. Explain the difference between observing something concerning and deciding what caused it.
4. Help the reader document specific observations rather than relying on vague impressions.
5. Discuss the importance of involving the parent in conversations rather than jumping immediately to control.
6. Clearly distinguish routine concerns from situations that require prompt professional evaluation or immediate emergency assistance.
7. Introduce the idea that the first job is understanding the situation—not trying to solve everything.

**Practical deliverable:** A one-page "What I've Noticed" worksheet recording observed changes, dates, concerns, and unanswered questions.

**Chapter boundary:** Do not diagnose dementia, explain disease progression, or provide detailed medical assessments. Those belong with qualified professionals and, where appropriate, later books.

**Reader's final outcome:** "I may not know exactly what's happening, but I can explain what I've noticed and identify what needs attention."

## Chapter 2 — Is This an Emergency or Just a Bad Week?

**Author direction (2026-11-02):**
- Preserve the red-yellow-green system.
- Ensure examples represent both cognitive and physical health changes.
- Where supported by reliable sources, include or strengthen examples involving: breathing difficulty; sudden weakness; falls and head injuries; uncontrolled or rapidly worsening symptoms; medication mistakes or reactions; dehydration or inability to eat and drink; changes after surgery or hospital discharge; sudden confusion or delirium.
- Do not create exhaustive medical symptom lists.
- Do not imply that the reader can diagnose a condition. The reader's responsibility is to notice change and contact the appropriate professional.
- Add a line directing families with recent discharge instructions to use the hospital's or clinician's after-hours number when appropriate.
- Preserve the instruction to call 911 when red-flag symptoms are present.

## Chapter 3 — Everyone Says "Get Organized," But Organize What?

**Author direction (2026-11-02):**
- Broaden the Seven-Area Aging Parent Assessment.
- Health and functioning sections must capture: current diagnoses; date and reason for the most recent hospitalization; recent surgery or procedure; treating specialists; treatment or therapy schedule; medication changes; discharge instructions; activity or lifting restrictions; PT, OT, or home-health involvement; mobility changes; pain, fatigue, appetite and hydration concerns; durable medical equipment; transportation needs; the parent's normal physical and mental baseline.
- Add discharge papers, treatment schedules and rehabilitation instructions to the records readers may need to locate.
- Use the diagnosis-first family to demonstrate gathering information without taking over (author chose a new family: the Lindqvists).
- Do not turn the assessment into a medical chart. Keep it usable by an ordinary family member.
