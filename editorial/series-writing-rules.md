# WHEN YOUR PARENTS NEED YOU
## Master Series Writing & Editorial Rules
**Version 1.0 — November 2026**

### Project mission

Create a 10-book nonfiction series for adult children, primarily Gen X and Millennials, who are beginning to manage the challenges of aging parents.

The series must be practical, reassuring without being sentimental, factually responsible, and genuinely useful. Its purpose is to help ordinary people become better organized, ask better questions, communicate more effectively, and know when to involve qualified professionals.

We are not writing medical textbooks, financial guides, legal manuals, inspirational self-help books, or memoirs.

**Our central promise:** Help readers figure out what to do next when their parents begin needing more help.

### 1. Author voice and personality

Use a balanced combination of:
- Natural storytelling and relatable observations.
- Practical instructions that readers can follow.
- Direct, conversational explanations.
- Occasional understated humor where appropriate.
- Respect for the emotional complexity of caring for parents.

Write like a thoughtful adult explaining something to another capable adult who is overwhelmed, not uninformed.

The author should sound observant, experienced in ordinary family life, and grounded. Never imply professional credentials that the author does not possess.

Avoid artificial intimacy, exaggerated compassion, motivational speeches, and emotional manipulation.

### 2. Write naturally — remove the AI feel

Avoid:
- Repetitive introductions and conclusions.
- Formulaic transitions and identical chapter structures.
- Overused phrases such as "navigate this journey," "you're not alone," "in today's fast-paced world," and "empower yourself."
- Excessive metaphors, slogans, and rhetorical questions.
- Repeatedly explaining why caregiving is difficult.
- Lists of generic recommendations that lack actionable substance.
- Overly polished sentences that sound more like corporate marketing than human writing.
- Summarizing information immediately after already explaining it.
- Unnecessary em dashes and excessive bold text.

Use varied sentence lengths, natural paragraph development, specific situations, and ordinary language.

No chapter should read as if it was assembled from an AI-generated checklist.

### 3. Personal stories and examples

Use authentic personal experiences only when their details have been confirmed by the author.

Never fabricate:
- Experiences involving the author's parents or family.
- Conversations that supposedly happened.
- Patient stories or testimonials.
- Professional opinions or interviews.
- Real-world outcomes attributed to specific individuals.

Hypothetical examples are allowed when clearly labeled or written in a way that makes their illustrative nature obvious.

Use stories only when they help explain a problem, clarify a decision, or make an instruction more understandable.

Do not force a personal story into every chapter.

### 4. Medical, legal, and financial boundaries

The author is not presenting themselves as a doctor, nurse, attorney, financial planner, or insurance specialist.

**Allowed:**
- Explaining how to organize information.
- Providing general background verified against reliable sources.
- Suggesting questions readers can ask qualified professionals.
- Helping families prepare for appointments and conversations.
- Explaining where to look for official resources.
- Providing checklists, planners, and recordkeeping tools.

**Not allowed:**
- Diagnosing conditions or recommending treatments.
- Telling readers to start, stop, or change medications.
- Determining whether someone is medically or cognitively capable of making decisions.
- Recommending investments, asset transfers, or financial strategies.
- Advising readers how to qualify for particular government benefits through financial maneuvers.
- Providing individualized legal guidance.
- Guaranteeing eligibility, coverage, costs, or outcomes.

Respect the parent's consent, privacy, independence, and decision-making rights. Do not assume adult children are automatically authorized to access records or make decisions.

Disclaimers do not excuse inaccurate or inappropriate advice.

### 5. Fact-checking and sourcing

Every material factual claim must be supportable.

Prioritize primary and authoritative sources, including as appropriate:
- National Institute on Aging (NIA).
- Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC).
- Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS).
- Administration for Community Living (ACL).
- Relevant state and local government agencies.
- Official healthcare, benefits, and consumer-protection resources.
- Qualified professional organizations and established research publications.

For any source-dependent claim:
1. Verify the information against the actual source.
2. Record the source name and URL.
3. Record the verification date.
4. Specify applicable location or jurisdiction.
5. Flag information that is likely to change.
6. Avoid overstating what the source establishes.

Use dated references such as "As of November 2026" only when the claim has actually been verified as of that date.

Do not invent citations, statistics, quotations, professional consensus, or study findings.

If a statement cannot be verified, remove it, qualify it appropriately, or mark it for editorial review. Never silently guess.

Use unobtrusive endnotes or a resources section rather than interrupting every paragraph with citations.

### 6. Reader-first chapter design

Every chapter must address a distinct reader problem.

The reader should finish the chapter understanding:
1. What problem they are facing.
2. Why that problem deserves attention.
3. What they can reasonably do about it.
4. What information they still need.
5. When to seek professional or outside assistance.
6. What useful next action they can take.

Do not write chapters simply to explore a topic academically.

Do not repeat previous chapters to increase word count.

### 7. Practical tools

Provide useful tools when they improve the chapter: checklists, question lists, conversation starters, planning worksheets, information organizers, decision comparison frameworks, realistic examples.

Every tool must serve a clear purpose. Avoid lengthy worksheets that simply restate the preceding text. Tools should be usable without the reader purchasing anything else.

### 8. Chapter structure

A guide, not a rigid formula:

- **Opening:** Begin with a recognizable frustration, situation, observation, or difficult question.
- **Development:** Explain the problem and provide meaningful guidance.
- **Application:** Demonstrate how the reader could put the guidance to work.
- **Practical resource:** Include an appropriate tool when useful.
- **Closing:** End with a concrete next action or natural transition.

Vary the presentation and pacing. Do not make every chapter begin or end identically.

### 9. Length and depth

Suggested starting target for Book 1: approximately 20,000–25,000 words across ten chapters, excluding supplemental materials. These are planning targets, not quotas. A shorter, useful chapter is preferable to a longer repetitive one. Expand through meaningful examples, decision-making guidance, and practical detail—not filler.

### 10. Emotional tone

Acknowledge the difficult parts of caregiving without dwelling on them. Treat parents as adults with preferences and rights; adult children as capable people learning unfamiliar responsibilities; family disagreements with fairness; illness, cognitive decline, disability, and end-of-life decisions with dignity.

Avoid depicting older adults as helpless or assuming aging automatically means cognitive impairment. Recognize that families have different resources, living arrangements, relationships, and cultural expectations.

### 11. Series continuity

1. **Your Parent Needs Help—Now What?** — The first 30 days and initial organization.
2. **What Do I Say Now?** — Memory changes, behavior, and communication.
3. **Is It Still Safe for Mom or Dad to Live Alone?** — Independence, home safety, and daily support.
4. **Keeping Track of Everything** — Healthcare information and appointment coordination.
5. **They're Sending Dad Home Tomorrow** — Hospital discharge and care transitions.
6. **Why Am I the Only One Helping?** — Siblings and family responsibility.
7. **Helping Mom and Dad from 500 Miles Away** — Long-distance caregiving.
8. **When Family Help Isn't Enough** — Finding and comparing outside care.
9. **Who Takes Care of You?** — Caregiver workload, boundaries, and sustainability.
10. **The Conversations We Keep Putting Off** — Preparing for decline, end-of-life conversations, and grief.

Working titles pending market and editorial validation. Each book must have a distinct problem, reader promise, and set of practical outcomes. Book 1 introduces major issues without replacing the detailed guidance of later books. Cross-reference other books only where genuinely useful. Never turn chapters into sales pitches.

### 12. United States focus

The initial audience is primarily readers in the United States. Do not assume state laws, healthcare systems, benefit programs, or support services are identical everywhere. When a subject depends on location, direct the reader toward relevant official sources or qualified local professionals.

### 13. Editorial quality control

Before submitting any chapter, review it for: natural human voice; clear purpose and reader benefit; repetition within the chapter and across the book; unverified claims and missing citations; medical, legal, and financial advice concerns; invented or overstated examples; weak practical guidance; inappropriate generalizations about older adults or families; unnecessary content and filler; consistency with the rest of the series.

Provide a separate editorial report identifying unresolved factual or professional-review concerns. Do not include internal notes in the finished manuscript.

### 14. Final publication standards

The completed manuscript must contain only material intended for readers. Remove AI instructions and prompt language, draft notes and editor comments, placeholder citations, duplicated sections, internal revision markers, artificial chapter conclusions, and unnecessary promotional language.

Include appropriate introductory disclosures, professional-advice boundaries, source notes, and resource references. Maintain a separate internal source-and-verification log.

**Final guiding principle:** Write the book we wish someone had handed us when we first realized our parents needed help: useful, straightforward, respectful, and worth the reader's time.
