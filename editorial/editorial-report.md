## Per-chapter review notes

### Chapter 1: Something Has Changed (draft 1, 2026-11-02)

- Length: about 3,050 words including the worksheet and notes (about 2,700 of prose). Slightly above a 2,000–2,500 per-chapter average; the urgency section is the one place not to cut.
- Outline coverage: all seven required points addressed (missed changes; six starting points; observing vs. explaining; documenting; involving the parent; urgency tiers; understanding first). Deliverable included as a one-page worksheet.
- Boundaries: no diagnosis, disease progression, or medication advice. Medication line explicitly tells readers not to change meds and to report to the prescriber.
- Autonomy and consent: covered in "Bring your parent into it" and the caution against covert checking of bank statements.
- Style: no em dashes; bold limited to section lead-ins, the urgency tiers, and the worksheet.

### Chapter 2: Is This an Emergency or Just a Bad Week? (draft 1, 2026-11-02)

- Length: about 2,980 words including tool and notes.
- Red-yellow-green system preserved from the previous draft, plus the "go one color up" tiebreaker.
- Requested examples covered: breathing difficulty (red); sudden weakness (red, via stroke signs); falls and head injuries (red and yellow, including blood thinners and delayed symptoms); uncontrolled or worsening symptoms (yellow, escalating to red); medication mistakes and reactions (yellow via Poison Help/prescriber, red for overdose with serious symptoms); dehydration and not eating or drinking (yellow and red); post-surgery and discharge changes (own section); sudden confusion and delirium (red and yellow).
- Cognitive and physical examples both present in each color.
- Required discharge after-hours line included verbatim in substance: "If your parent was recently discharged and has instructions, use the hospital's or clinician's after-hours number when the instructions say to, or when something worries you but isn't a red flag." Followed by "For red-flag symptoms, don't wait for a callback: call 911."
- 911 instruction for red flags preserved throughout.
- Reader's role framed as noticing change and contacting the right professional, stated explicitly in the opening, after the "why sorting is hard" section, and in the worked example.
- Lists kept illustrative, not exhaustive.
- Worked example is clearly labeled as made up.

### Chapter 3: Everyone Says "Get Organized," But Organize What? (draft 1, 2026-11-02)

- Seven-Area Assessment kept and broadened. Health section captures: current diagnoses, most recent hospitalization (date, hospital, reason), recent surgery or procedure, treating specialists, treatment or therapy schedule, discharge instructions, activity/lifting/driving restrictions, PT/OT/home health. Medications section captures recent changes. Daily functioning captures baseline, mobility changes, pain, fatigue, appetite, hydration, everyday tasks, and durable medical equipment. Transportation captures appointment and treatment transport and post-surgery driving limits.
- Records list adds discharge papers, treatment/therapy schedules, and rehab/home-exercise instructions.
- Plain-language guardrail stated explicitly near the start.
- Lindqvist family shows gathering without taking over: copies papers rather than reading them in front of her, asks what the doctor said, asks to attend, accepts "not yet" on portal access, records baseline; remote sibling contributes by phone.

### Chapter 4: What's Actually Going On? (draft 1, 2026-11-02)

- Outline coverage: why views differ (time slices, parent behaves differently by person, distance, thresholds, stakes, parent's own view); four kinds of information (saw / told / concluded / assumed or feared) with a worked table; six daily-life areas (meals, transportation, household, social, mobility, appointments); asking without interrogating (routine questions, what's working, parent's view first, side-by-side, one topic, no quizzing); blind spots from distance and short visits; documenting respectfully and privacy (need-to-know, group texts, parent may read it, preferences in their words); unknowns and who can clarify (parent, doctor, pharmacist, people who see them with consent, Area Agency on Aging/Eldercare Locator, geriatric care manager).
- Boundary stated twice: not a formal clinical, cognitive, or home-safety assessment.
- Deliverable: Current Situation Snapshot with observations (with source tags), parent preferences, unknowns, who can clarify, agreement/disagreement, and next steps.
- Only two source-dependent claims (Eldercare Locator, geriatric care manager costs), both logged.

### Chapter 5: Making Sense of the Appointments (draft 1, 2026-11-02)

- Outline coverage: why information gets lost (multiple providers, short visits, setting, "it went fine," telephone effect, unclear follow-up ownership); concise question prep (top three, parent's goals first, what's changed); recording accurately (doctor said vs. my understanding, exact names, action items, after-visit summary); permission and authorization (verbal in room, written release at each office, HIPAA personal representative, privacy within permission); tracking (one calendar, contact list, open-questions list, note-taker, update template); clarifying confusing instructions (call, be specific, pharmacist, tell both providers if they conflict, don't change meds); who to contact (table).
- Boundary: explicit "what your role isn't" list; no treatment, medication, or diagnostic advice. Book 4 referenced for the full system.
- Deliverable: reusable Appointment Preparation and Follow-Up Worksheet (Before / During / After).
